"Only Pardoned": Alien Rule and the Suppression of Chechen and Ingush National Life (1965-1989)

In 1965-1989 the Chechens and the Ingush, whom the coloniser had merged into a single region (the Chechen-Ingush ASSR), were ruled by Russian officials sent from Moscow. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: "in the early 1970s, of the 60 ministries and agencies of the Council of Ministers of the ChIASSR only 17 were headed by Chechens or Ingush. In most rural districts the first secretaries of the district committees were Russians"[1]. In 1972 Chechen and Ingush communists wrote to Moscow about this twice. From 16 to 19 January 1973 several thousand Ingush (participants speak of 15 thousand), joined by Chechens, rallied on the central square of Grozny in front of the building of the region's occupation administration and demanded the return of the Prigorodny district, handed to North Ossetia during the deportation of 1944, or the lifting of the ban on settling there. On the night of 19 January soldiers of the internal troops (predecessors of today's Rosgvardiya) with truncheons and firemen spraying icy water from hoses dispersed those who remained on the square, drove them out of the city and dropped them off in the frost[2]. Then the coloniser went after those who had complained: on 13 March 1973 Moscow adopted a resolution "On Anti-Social Nationalist Manifestations in the City of Grozny", the authors of the letters were expelled from the only permitted party, the organisers of the rally were put on trial[2], and in December KGB chairman Andropov (the KGB being the predecessor of today's FSB) reported on "measures" against "about 400 Ingush"[3]. In those same years the coloniser worked to leave the Chechens and the Ingush without a history, a faith, a language or a livelihood of their own. The USSR rewrote history, imposing its own concept, "the 200th anniversary of the voluntary entry of Chechen-Ingushetia into Russia", and persecuted the Chechen historians who disputed it through decisions of the region's occupation administration[4]. In a quarter of a century the occupier did not allow the highlanders to open a single mosque[5]. The schools to which Chechen and Ingush children returned worked only in Russian from the first grade: the law of the RSFSR of 16 April 1959 declared that "instruction at school is conducted in the pupils' native language" and left the choice of language to parents, but after the return from exile the coloniser opened no schools with Chechen as the language of instruction, so there was nothing to choose from[6]. The factories and oil enterprises of Grozny hired newcomers rather than Chechens: by the end of the 1980s only 16 % of oil-refining workers were Chechens or Ingush, according to official statistics of 1979 up to 28 % of the able-bodied lived off their household plots, and according to the census of 1989 almost a quarter of the people lived outside the republic[1]. The coloniser said aloud what it thought of the people after the dispersal. A. Martazanova, a participant in the events of 1973, recalls: at a meeting of the region's occupation administration on 27 March one of its leaders, Bokov, "said that the Ingush and the Chechens had not yet been rehabilitated, that they had only been pardoned in advance, in the hope of good behaviour", while Rustem Goygov, head of a department in the same administration, who began: "But what about the decisions of the 20th Congress of the CPSU [the congress of 1956 that condemned the deportations of peoples], which…", was not allowed to finish and a month later was removed from his post; the phrase had first been uttered by Solomentsev, head of the government of the RSFSR, who had come to Grozny[2]. Eyewitness Aza Bazorkina recalls that a few days after the dispersal the newspaper Grozny Worker printed, over the signatures of intimidated Ingush, an article "To Serve One's People Honestly", which "says that the Ingush people have not been rehabilitated (as if there had been no 20th Party Congress!) but pardoned"[7].

T0151 Imposition of Alien Governance
January 1965
C1182.T0151 →

G0010 USSR
S0008 Government

After the return of the Chechens and the Ingush from exile the USSR did not let the indigenous inhabitants hold key leading posts in government and industry, reserving them for Russian settlers. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: "The leading posts (first secretary of the obkom of the CPSU [head of the region's occupation administration], minister of the MVD, republican prosecutor, head of the KGB [predecessor of today's FSB], minister of finance, secretary of the Grozny gorkom of the CPSU [head of the city] and chairman of the Grozny gorispolkom [head of the city administration], head of Grozneft, military commissar of the republic and some others) are reserved for Russian officials"; for the Chechens and the Ingush, 70 % of the population, "roughly 30% of posts were reserved"[1]. According to the letter of 26 communists of 1972, which the historians quote, "in the early 1970s, of the 60 ministries and agencies of the Council of Ministers of the ChIASSR [the Chechen-Ingush republic] only 17 were headed by Chechens or Ingush. In most rural districts the first secretaries of the raikoms [heads of districts] were Russians"[1]. The historians write: "That is precisely why the CC of the CPSU [the central authority in Moscow] keeps sending new first secretaries of the obkom [heads of the region] to Chechen-Ingushetia" - in 1966-1989 these were Apryatkin, Vlasov and Foteev, sent in from other regions[1]. When the Ingush at the rally of January 1973 demanded that locals be appointed to leading posts, Bokov, a leader in the region's occupation administration, replied at a meeting of party functionaries on 21 January: "In their speeches they went so far as to say that a measure should be carried out here to take account of leading cadres from among the Chechens and the Ingush, that is, to carry out indigenisation"[2]. Historian A. B. Kuznetsova writes, citing the witness B. Kh. Toriev: "Chechens and Ingush were subjected to harsh discrimination in admission to the CPSU - they were admitted only from among the workers, so that those who held engineering positions were forced to go and work at a factory as labourers in order to be able to join the party"[8]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that only by the mid-1980s did Chechens and Ingush head 155 of the 194 village soviets and 11 of the 19 city and district soviets, while "the most significant posts at the middle and republican level, as well as the leadership of Grozny and of the regional committee of the CPSU, remained with the Russian nomenklatura"[1]. The historians write that on 1 July 1989 the region's occupation administration at its meeting "for the first time in the entire history of the Chechen-Ingush party organisation rejected the candidate proposed by the CC of the CPSU (N. I. Semenov) and elected Doku Zavgaev first secretary"[1].

T0021 Neutralization of the Opposition
January 1965
C1182.T0021 →

G0010 USSR
S0008 Government

In 1972-1981 the Chechens and the Ingush twice wrote to Moscow about discrimination and protested on the square; each time the coloniser expelled from the only permitted party and put on trial the protesters, not the region's occupation leadership. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that in March 1972 26 Chechen and Ingush communists "wrote a collective letter addressed to the CC of the CPSU [the central authority in Moscow], in which they directly accused the party leadership of Chechen-Ingushetia not only of chauvinism but also of corruption. The result of the appeal, however, was not what the authors of the collective letter had counted on: it was not the leaders of the republic who were punished, but they themselves"; S. Bokaev, S. Pliev, N. Umarkhadzhaev, B. Kostoev and B. Lyanov were expelled from the party[1]. On 16 January 1973 the Ingush gathered on the central square of Grozny, in front of the building of the region's occupation administration, and Chechens joined them; rally participant Salman Mamatiev recalls: "on the second day there were almost as many Chechens on the square as Ingush"[2]. Eyewitness Aza Bazorkina recalls: "From a makeshift rostrum on Lenin Square, on 16, 17 and 18 January, day and night, the grievance of dispossessed, deceived people pours out", and on the night of 19 January the militia and the internal troops (predecessors of today's Rosgvardiya) cleared the square: "The doubting young people, who did not want to disperse without reliable information, were beaten with rubber truncheons and doused with icy water from fire hoses, herded into buses, driven out of the city and let out there, soaking wet, into a twenty-degree frost in an open field"[7]. On 13 March 1973 Moscow adopted a resolution "On Anti-Social Nationalist Manifestations in the City of Grozny"; on 27 March the region's occupation administration resolved to accept it "for unswerving guidance and execution" and recorded: "For nationalist actions and active participation in anti-social manifestations the following have been expelled from the party: Pliev S. Kh., Gazdiev A. M., as well as Kushtov A. S. - physician at city hospital No. 2, Abadiev B. - director of the puppet theatre, Uzhakhova - teacher at boarding school No. 15, Egiev - former chairman of the construction committee of SMU-7 [trade-union committee of a construction directorate], Daurbekov - former head of department at the Nazran district newspaper"[2]. Historian A. B. Kuznetsova gives the tally: "23 people were expelled from the CPSU, 18 from the VLKSM [the Komsomol, the youth organisation of Soviet power], 9 people were removed from leading positions and 14 people were prosecuted"[8]. On 27 December 1973 KGB chairman Andropov (the KGB being the predecessor of today's FSB) reported to Moscow: "After the well-known events that took place in the city of Grozny in January 1973, in which about 400 Ingush from the Prigorodny district of the North Ossetian ASSR took an active part, as a result of measures taken by the state security organs new attempts to carry out socially dangerous acts, especially in February 1973 on the anniversary of the eviction of the Ingush and also on the eve of the holidays of 1 and 9 May, were prevented"[3]. In June 1981 23 communists wrote to Brezhnev about the corruption of Vlasov, head of the region's occupation administration; historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: "The CC of the CPSU this time too backed the republican leadership, accusing the authors of the letter of nationalism (although among its signatories were people of various nationalities, including Russians and Ukrainians). Two people were expelled from the party"[1].

T0004 Rewriting of History
January 1965
C1182.T0004 →

G0010 USSR
S0012 Occupation and Controlled Administrations

From 1973 the coloniser justified the deportation and did not let the Chechens speak of it or of the military seizure of the region, and from 1979 it imposed a "concept" of "the 200th anniversary of the voluntary entry of Chechen-Ingushetia into Russia" and persecuted the historians who disputed it. At a meeting of the region's occupation administration on 27 March 1973 one of its leaders, Bokov, justified the eviction of 1944: "All this, in our view, forced the government to take this extreme measure in order to put an end to the anti-Soviet actions of the political bandit groups [the authorities' name for armed opponents], which found shelter among a significant part of the Chechen-Ingush population", and the minutes of the same meeting named the bearers of "nationalist" ideas one by one: "the writer Khalid Oshaev, expelled from the party, the former deputy director of a plant Kostoev B., the university lecturer and candidate of sciences Vagapov Ya."[2]. Historian A. B. Kuznetsova writes that at the discussion of the two-volume "Essays on the History of the Chechen-Ingush ASSR" in 1973 the speakers criticised the "belittling" of the role of the Russian people and the works of the historian V. I. Filkin, "while not a word was said at the discussion about the deportation"[8]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that the region's occupation administration launched a persecution of the Chechen intelligentsia "under the guise of a discussion around the 'concept' of the 200th anniversary of the voluntary entry of Chechen-Ingushetia into Russia", whose author was considered to be the archaeologist Vinogradov, and he "relied even on the authority of the CC of the CPSU [the central authority in Moscow]"[1]. In February 1979 L. I. Lavrov, a historian of the Academy of Sciences, wrote on instructions from his superiors a review of the "Justification Memorandum" for the jubilee: the oaths of the 18th century "did not lead to the actual entry of the region into the Russian Empire, since even after the oaths no officials of the tsarist administration appeared there", and "the planned celebration of the jubilee, in the light of the above, appears historically unjustified"; historian V. P. Krikunov, who published the memorandum in 1990, writes: "The first copy of the memorandum got lost and, when the question of whether or not to celebrate the 200th 'jubilee' was being decided, was not taken into account (or rather, it was taken into account, and that, evidently, is why it got lost)"[9]. Historian Viktor Shnirelman writes: "In those years Vinogradov became the chief adviser of the obkom of the CPSU [the region's occupation administration] on ideology and social sciences, which in effect made him the first censor of scholarly and publishing activity in the republic"; "Such Chechen and Ingush historians as M. N. Muzaev, A. Z. Vatsuev, Ya. Z. Akhmadov, Kh. Akiev, Ya. S. Vagapov, Kh. S. Akhmadov and others were subjected to persecution"; the 1977 book by the linguist Kyuri Chokaev, "The Language and History of the Vainakhs", "was declared in the obkom… a model of 'rabid nationalism', and the entire print run was destroyed on the spot"[10]. Historians D. B. Abdurakhmanov and Ya. Z. Akhmadov also write that for disagreeing with the "concept" "there were officially pilloried [publicly disgraced] … the Chechen-Ingush State University lecturer F. P. Bokov, professors N. P. Gritsenko and V. P. Krikunov"[4]. Shnirelman writes that Vlasov, head of the region's occupation administration, reproduced the concept "in his speech at the III plenum of the Chechen-Ingush obkom of the CPSU on 14 July 1979" and "gave instructions to propagate it widely in the local media", and in 1982 the jubilee of the "voluntary entry" was combined with the 60th anniversary of the republic: "On this occasion the republic was awarded the Order of the Red Banner of Labour; its leaders received awards too. In particular, Vlasov was presented with the Order of Lenin"[10]. On 29 June 1984 the region's occupation administration recorded in its minutes on the work of the Institute of History, Sociology and Philology: "Vagapov Ya. S., Akhmadov Ya. Z., Vatsuev A. Z. permit themselves politically harmful judgements, dispute the scholarly validity of the concept of the voluntary entry of the Chechens and the Ingush into Russia"[4]. Historians D. B. Abdurakhmanov and Ya. Z. Akhmadov write that only in 1990 did "the same organ of the CPSU revoke its decision concerning the institute and its staff as mistaken"[4].

T0100 Seizure of Religious Institutions
January 1965

S0008 Government

After the return of the Chechens from exile the coloniser did not allow a single mosque to be opened and, through its officials, decided who could pray and where. Muzarov, an official supervising religion, wrote in a report of 25 August 1983: "In Chechen-Ingushetia in the pre-October period [before 1917] Muslim ideology reigned supreme. There were 2,675 mosques, 60 thousand murids [members of religious brotherhoods], 38 sheikhs [religious mentors, heads of brotherhoods], 2,850 mullahs [Islamic clergymen] and 140 religious schools. After the restoration of the republic's autonomy in 1957 it so happened that on the territory of the Chechen-Ingush ASSR there was not a single mosque"[5]. The mosques did not vanish by themselves. Most of them the coloniser had closed before the war: historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that religious schools "after the closure of most mosques… moved into the homes of mullahs and Islamic scholars"[1]. Historian Dalkhan Khozhaev writes that during the exile "medieval crypts and pre-revolutionary mosques, tombs and shrines… were blown up"[7]; historians Akhmadov and Khasmagomadov add that in Grozny "the city mosque was destroyed"[1]. The surviving buildings the coloniser took over as clubs and warehouses. On 21 January 1967 277 believers of the Nazran district submitted an application to open a mosque in the village of Barsuki; Asaulka, an official supervising religion, reported to Moscow that the district administration, by its decision of 10 June 1967, had refused "to consider the application on the grounds that, as established by an inspection, 'many signatures under the application were made not by the persons indicated in the application but forged, and the surnames of some persons are fictitious'"[11]. On 11 November 1969 the government of the region, subordinate to Moscow, in a resolution "On Measures to Strengthen Control over Observance of the Legislation on Religious Cults", recorded that in the districts "ministers of the cult in a number of cases arbitrarily engage in building and opening prayer houses, hold collective rites in public places, organise pilgrimages of believers to so-called 'holy places'", while "effective measures aimed at the gradual cessation of their activity are not being taken"[12]. On 18 May 1977 Bobov, an official supervising religion, reported to Kerimov, the head of that government: "in the republic there are no Muslim religious associations or ministers of the cult registered with the authorities, while at the same time, contrary to the law, 263 Muslim religious societies and groups operate unofficially"[13]. On 19 August 1980 Bobov reported to Moscow: in the republic "there are 62 buildings of former mosques… 17 used as clubs and schools, 25 as storage premises, 8 as factory workshops, post offices, shops, 2 handed over to the use of believers"[14]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that the region's occupation authorities "directly forbade the opening of mosques in Chechen and Ingush villages, and did not allow the numerous murid [religious] communities to perform religious rites openly either"[1].

T0116.004 Ban on the Indigenous People's Religious Practice
January 1965

In 1958-1989 the coloniser declared Chechen shrines closed, put people on trial for printing the Quran and kept believers on file. On 10 December 1958 the Moscow agency supervising religion sent its officials in the regions a circular headed thus: "On Measures to Stop Pilgrimage to So-Called 'Holy Places'"; its task: "to achieve the cessation of pilgrimage and the closure of so-called 'holy places'"[15]. In Chechnya this circular fell upon the grave of the mother of Sheikh Kunta-Haji, founder of the largest Chechen brotherhood. The same agency's report for 1973 says: the grave "was declared 'closed' by the local authorities about fourteen years ago. However, from 1961 the religious activists began restoring it without permission… In 1970-1971 this so-called 'holy place'… was visited by over 10 thousand people… On the way the pilgrims, often with the participation of adolescents, perform the collective religious rite of 'zikr' [a joint prayer with circular movement and chanting]"[16]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: "The performance of the 'loud' zikr [prayer aloud] of the Qadiri tariqa [brotherhood] (the order of Kunta-Haji) was suppressed with particular severity"[1]. On 29 June 1979 the Oktyabrsky district court of Grozny tried Alaudin Bogatyrev, Salaudin Dubaev and Anatoly Yushkin; the verdict states that Bogatyrev "with the aim of engaging in a prohibited trade… was engaged in the production by printing methods, on a significant scale, of literature of religious content in the Arabic language": from 1971 to 1978 they had clandestinely printed 2,500 copies of the Quran, 600 copies of parts of it and 500 Arabic textbooks, and the court ruled "to subject" Bogatyrev "to 6 years of deprivation of liberty with confiscation of property, to be served in a strict-regime corrective labour colony", and Dubaev and Yushkin to four years each[17]. On 5 April 1983 Moscow adopted a resolution "On Measures for the Ideological Isolation of the Reactionary Part of the Muslim Clergy"; Muzarov, the official supervising religion in the region, reported on 25 August 1983: "59 murid [religious] groups and societies have organisationally ceased their activity and the activity of more than 100 ministers of the cult [mullahs] has been neutralised… Work was carried out to put all extremist elements and fanatics on file, a map of murid [religious] organisations was drawn up"; "The number of murids [members of brotherhoods] has fallen - from 20 thousand to 9 thousand"[5]. The Chechens did not submit: on 5 May 1989 the agency supervising religion counted 311 brotherhood groups in the republic, "uniting about 8,300 people", of which "the 'Kunta-Haji' brotherhood - 183 groups"[18].

T0117.001 Migratory Replacement
January 1965
C1182.T0117.001 →

G0010 USSR
S0008 Government

In 1965-1989 the coloniser brought workers from Russia to the factories of Grozny and gave them housing, while pushing the Chechens out of the republic to seek work and permanent residence in other regions. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: "in the 30 years since the return of the Chechens and the Ingush to their Homeland, only 16% of the workers of the oil-refining and petrochemical enterprises of Chechen-Ingushetia were Chechens or Ingush", in the radio-engineering industry 14 %, in instrument-making 6 %, while the missing workers and engineers "were brought in from Russian regions by way of organised recruitment. Moreover, some specialists received the keys to their flats right at the railway station"[1]. The historians write: "Every year more than 100 thousand people, organised in construction brigades, left Chechen-Ingushetia for construction work"[1]; historians D. B. Abdurakhmanov and Ya. Z. Akhmadov write that a family of five or six, on a state-farm worker's wage of 60-80 roubles, was left with "either 'shabashka' - hard 14-16-hour labour on construction sites in rural districts of the country, or departure for permanent residence in Kalmykia, the Rostov, Astrakhan and Volgograd regions to work on livestock farms"[4]. From the second half of the 1980s the region's occupation administration, under agreements with the Kalinin, Novgorod and Omsk regions, sent villagers to settle in the Non-Black-Earth zone[1]. The census of 1989 counted 956,879 Chechens in the USSR, of whom 734,501 lived in Chechen-Ingushetia: almost every fourth lived outside the republic[19]; the historians write: "the main cause of migration was unemployment"[1].

T0032 Legal Segregation of the Population
January 1965
C1182.T0032 →

G0010 USSR

In 1965-1989 the coloniser, by law and by administrative decisions, kept the Chechens out of the cities and factories of their own republic. The coloniser had restricted the residence registration of Chechens in Grozny as early as 1958; historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: "these restrictions in fact ceased to operate only in the 1980s"[1]. Historian A. B. Kuznetsova writes that in the early 1970s "the leadership of the ChIASSR [the Chechen-Ingush republic] (in agreement with the union centre [Moscow]) declared that the labour resources of the republic (read: the unemployed) had to be used locally"[8]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: "Already in 1979 up to 28% of all able-bodied inhabitants of the Chechen-Ingush ASSR, according to official statistics, derived their main income from work on their personal household plots, that is, were in fact unemployed. Ten years later the level of hidden unemployment, by some estimates, had risen to 40%"[1].

T0114.003 Conversion of Schools to the Metropole's Language
January 1965
C1182.T0114.003 →
C1182 "Only Pardoned": Alien Rule and the Suppression of Chechen and Ingush National Life (1965-1989)
G0010 USSR
S0008 Government

After the return of the Chechens from exile the coloniser switched all the schools of the republic to Russian from the first grade, reduced the Chechen language to a few lessons a week and in the schools of Grozny did not teach it at all. The law of the RSFSR of 16 April 1959 declared that "instruction at school is conducted in the pupils' native language" and left the choice of language to parents, but after the return from exile the coloniser opened no schools with Chechen as the language of instruction, so there was nothing to choose from[6]. Sociolinguist Musa Ovkhadov writes: "After the restoration of the autonomy of Chechen-Ingushetia Russian became the language of instruction in the schools of the republic from grade 1"; "In the schools of the capital of the republic, the city of Grozny, until the end of the 1980s the Chechen language was not taught (in 1989 121 thousand Chechens lived in Grozny out of its population of 400 thousand)"[20]. Educationalist Isita Muskhanova writes: "teaching in all the schools of Chechen-Ingushetia was conducted in Russian from the first to the last year of study, while the study of the native language and native literature was allotted only a few hours a week"; the republican newspapers of the first half of the 1960s wrote that to return to the national school "would mean taking a step backwards"[21]. Philologist Zulay Khamidova, who spent nine years fighting for Chechen lessons in city schools, recalls: "In kindergartens the language of upbringing was Russian, and if a child knew only the native language, he was punished and required to speak only Russian"; after her efforts the Chechen and Ingush languages were introduced in city schools "as a subject, 1 hour a week, whereas 5 hours a week were allotted to Russian"[22]. By Musa Ovkhadov's count, the entire school course of Chechen language and literature from grade 1 to grade 10 in the 1970s-1980s amounted to 1,497 hours, on average 3.5 hours a week[20]. The result is visible in the official statistics of the USSR: in the Chechen and Ingush languages 154 books and pamphlets were published in 1940, 49 in 1970, 37 in 1980 and 39 in 1985[20]; the republic's television in the 1980s broadcast in Chechen for 20-25 minutes a day[23]. The Chechens did not give up their language: according to the census of 1989, 938,765 of the 956,879 Chechens named Chechen as their native language[8].
