Conversion of Schools to the Metropole's Language

T0114.003 Persistence Nokhchi (Chechens), Ukrainians

As part of educational assimilation, aggressors may carry out a total, forced conversion of the teaching process in all educational institutions to the colonizer's language. Depriving the Indigenous people of the right to educate their children in their native language leads to a rapid rupture of cultural continuity and the complete integration of the local population into the aggressor's information space.

Procedures Carried Out4

1769–1786 Russification of Education and Religion Ukrainians

Russification of Education and Religion (1769–1786) Russian Empire Government

The Russification of teaching in the country's principal institution of learning: from 1784 metropolitan Samuil Myslavsky introduced new classes of geography and history at the Kyiv-Mohyla Academy with an order to conduct them "in pure Russian," and by his order of 26 October 1784 demanded that teachers and students observe "the rules of orthography proper to the Russian language" - teachers faced dismissal for lapses, and students expulsion from the clerical estate[1]. The unification was completed by the order of February 13, 1786: "to adopt immediately for the Kyiv Academy the mode of instruction legalized for all schools in the All-Russian Empire"[2] – the Kyiv-Mohyla Academy was transferred to the empire-wide mode of teaching.

1919 Soviet-Ukrainian War: Second Invasion and Resource Depletion Ukrainians

Soviet-Ukrainian War: Second Invasion and Resource Depletion (1919) White Movement (AFSR) Occupation and Controlled Administrations

On 3 August 1919 general Mai-Maevsky, head of the Whites' administration in Katerynoslav, Kharkiv, Kursk and Poltava provinces, issued in Kharkiv order No. 22, opening it thus: "I announce the basic provisions adopted by the Special Council on the question of 'Ukrainian schools.'" The first point was reversal: "All schools in which, before the appearance of the 'Ukrainian' authorities, instruction was conducted in Russian, and then, by order of the Ukrainian authority, the Little Russian language was made the language of instruction, must return again to instruction in Russian"[3].

The history and geography of Ukraine the order struck from the curricula: "The teaching of 'Ukrainian studies' (the history and geography of Ukraine) is abolished"[3].

Nine days later the commander-in-chief confirmed the same norm: in the address "To the Population of Little Russia" Denikin declared Russian the state language "over the whole expanse of Russia," leaving to Ukrainian the private schools and the first years of primary school - "to ease the pupils' assimilation of the first rudiments of knowledge"[4]. In Kyiv order No. 22 was applied in the autumn as well: a summary of the Soviet side on the situation in the city records that "in all schools the Russian language is being introduced as the state language"[5].

1937–1939 NKVD National Operations and Institutional Russification Ukrainians

NKVD National Operations and Institutional Russification (1937–1939) USSR Government

On 24 January 1938 the Orgburo of the Central Committee of the VKP(b), by its resolution "On the Liquidation of National Schools and National Sections in Schools," closed non-Russian schools throughout the Union, liquidating the national districts and village soviets on the same day[6][7]; on 13 March the resolution of the Central Committee of the VKP(b) and the USSR Council of People's Commissars No. 324 "On the Compulsory Study of the Russian Language in the Schools of the National Republics and Regions" made the metropole's language a compulsory subject in all schools of the republics[8]. Kyiv executed the decision on 10 April 1938: the Politburo of the Central Committee of the KP(b)U, "proceeding from the decision of the Central Committee of the VKP(b)," declared "the further existence of special national schools inexpedient and harmful" and resolved to reorganize the German, Polish, Czech, Swedish and other schools and technical colleges "into Soviet schools of the ordinary type," to liquidate national sections and classes at ordinary schools, colleges and universities, and, where there was nowhere to transfer the pupils, to transfer the institutions themselves to ordinary school curricula - "with instruction in Ukrainian or Russian"; the Odesa German Pedagogical Institute was turned into an institute of foreign languages, and all the work was ordered finished by 1 August 1938[9]. The Politburo wrote its justification into the resolution itself: "enemies of the people - Trotskyites, Bukharinites and bourgeois nationalists operating in the People's Commissariat of Education of the Ukrainian SSR - implanted special national German, Polish, Czech, Swedish, Greek and other schools, turning them into hotbeds of bourgeois-nationalist, anti-Soviet influence on children"[9]. The decision was given the form of a state act by the Council of People's Commissars of the Ukrainian SSR in its resolution of 29 June 1938 on the reorganization of special national schools, colleges and the Odesa German Pedagogical Institute: by 1 August 1938, 766 national primary, incomplete secondary and secondary schools were subject to reorganization into ordinary schools with (predominantly) Russian and Ukrainian as the language of instruction, and another 122 were merged with Russian and Ukrainian ones on the territorial principle - the count of the historian Liudmyla Yakubova[10].

1965–1989 "Only Pardoned": Alien Rule and the Suppression of Chechen and Ingush National Life Nokhchi (Chechens)

"Only Pardoned": Alien Rule and the Suppression of Chechen and Ingush National Life (1965-1989) USSR Government

After the return of the Chechens from exile the coloniser switched all the schools of the republic to Russian from the first grade, reduced the Chechen language to a few lessons a week and in the schools of Grozny did not teach it at all. The law of the RSFSR of 16 April 1959 declared that "instruction at school is conducted in the pupils' native language" and left the choice of language to parents, but after the return from exile the coloniser opened no schools with Chechen as the language of instruction, so there was nothing to choose from[11]. Sociolinguist Musa Ovkhadov writes: "After the restoration of the autonomy of Chechen-Ingushetia Russian became the language of instruction in the schools of the republic from grade 1"; "In the schools of the capital of the republic, the city of Grozny, until the end of the 1980s the Chechen language was not taught (in 1989 121 thousand Chechens lived in Grozny out of its population of 400 thousand)"[12]. Educationalist Isita Muskhanova writes: "teaching in all the schools of Chechen-Ingushetia was conducted in Russian from the first to the last year of study, while the study of the native language and native literature was allotted only a few hours a week"; the republican newspapers of the first half of the 1960s wrote that to return to the national school "would mean taking a step backwards"[13]. Philologist Zulay Khamidova, who spent nine years fighting for Chechen lessons in city schools, recalls: "In kindergartens the language of upbringing was Russian, and if a child knew only the native language, he was punished and required to speak only Russian"; after her efforts the Chechen and Ingush languages were introduced in city schools "as a subject, 1 hour a week, whereas 5 hours a week were allotted to Russian"[14]. By Musa Ovkhadov's count, the entire school course of Chechen language and literature from grade 1 to grade 10 in the 1970s-1980s amounted to 1,497 hours, on average 3.5 hours a week[12]. The result is visible in the official statistics of the USSR: in the Chechen and Ingush languages 154 books and pamphlets were published in 1940, 49 in 1970, 37 in 1980 and 39 in 1985[12]; the republic's television in the 1980s broadcast in Chechen for 20-25 minutes a day[15]. The Chechens did not give up their language: according to the census of 1989, 938,765 of the 956,879 Chechens named Chechen as their native language[16].
