Hostage-Taking

Aggressors may carry out the forcible capture and detention of civilians or non-combatants. These actions are used for the operational neutralization of defenses (blackmailing resistance forces, shielding troops). At the consolidation stage, this technique may become institutionalized as a system of taking members of the elites and high-ranking figures (the "amanat" system), whose detention serves as the state's guarantee of the local population's loyalty and of the prevention of anti-colonial revolts.

From spring until the end of summer 1829, Commander-in-Chief Paskevich forcibly kept with the field army the Chechen elders who had been summoned for negotiations, directly calling them hostages. September 1836
C1163.T0099 →
C1163 The amanat-and-tribute system of colonel Pullo in the seized auls of Chechnya (1836-1840)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

In 1836-1840 Pullo, commander of the Sunzha Line and then of the left flank of the Caucasus Line (a chain of imperial fortifications on the land of the Nokhchi), made hostage-taking the load-bearing institution of the amanat-and-tribute system in the auls of Chechnya seized by the empire. An amanat is a person whom the empire took away from a family or community as a hostage; the threat of reprisal against the taken one held the relatives and the whole aul in subjection. In a memorandum of 1840 Pullo described the method: "I took amanats not indiscriminately, as was done before, but from the most respected or least reliable families, and by this means many auls plotting revolt were repeatedly kept in obedience" - and the scale of the institution: "up to 20 thousand rubles... were spent yearly on the upkeep of amanats"[12]. The taking of hostages went in waves as the troops advanced: the imperial historian Yurov recorded of December 1839 that under the pressure of the detachment even the villages that barred the entrance to the Argun gorge "humbled themselves, handed over amanats" - the troops took people as a "token of submission"[13]. Yurov described the conditions in which the hostages were kept: "prisoners and even amanats were treated inhumanly, kept in a damp pit, where they often fell ill"[13]. In the same memorandum Pullo demanded that hostageship be made perpetual: for the offenses of those under warranty, amanats "must without fail, according to their age, be transferred into cantonists or convict companies, replacing the departed with other amanats" - child hostages to be handed into the cantonists (a military estate into which the empire forcibly enrolled minors), adults into convict companies (forced labor under military escort); in the memorandum of 1840 this is recorded as a plan[12].

C1165.T0099 →
C1165 "Constriction of Chechnya from all sides": blockade, secret war, punitive expeditions and the Sunzha Line (1840-1846)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

Hostages were taken not as punishment for a rising but on suspicion - before an aul had done anything. On 9 January 1842 the commander of the Sunzha Line, in a report to general Olshevsky, commander of the left flank of the Caucasus Line (the chain of imperial fortifications on the land of the Nokhchi), described his order for the Kumyk plain (the lowland to the north-east of Chechnya): ""I instructed Colonel Freitag... if there is a suspicion that some village wants to flee, then at once to take from it a large number of amanats, so that the inhabitants themselves should feed them"" (an amanat is a person whom the empire took from a community as a hostage; the threat of reprisal against the one taken held the family and the whole aul in submission)[12]. The same report admits what the empire holds the people with: ""Winter and the amanats restrain them, at the present time, from fleeing to Chechnya""[12]. Hostages from Vainakh communities were also taken by the empire's civil administration in Georgia - on the mere news of a defection to the resistance. On 26 May 1841 the civil governor of Georgia-Imereti reported to Brayko, administrator of the civil department of the Transcaucasian region, that on news of the defection of the Kistins (a Vainakh community on the border with Georgia) to the side of Shamil (the imam, leader of the resistance of the highlanders of Dagestan and Chechnya to the Russian conquest), provincial secretary Limmerman instructed the district assessors (officials of the district administration) ""to detain here, under a plausible pretext, all the Kistins who wintered in Tusheti and on the Alvani field"" (Tusheti and the Alvani field are a mountain region and a plain in north-eastern Georgia)[12]. Those detained were not the ones who had defected but those who had come for the winter; the pretext for the detention is called ""plausible"" by the document itself.

C0074.T0099 →
C0074 Soviet-Ukrainian War: Second Invasion and Resource Depletion (1919)
G0013 Soviet Russia (RSFSR)
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

On 25 May 1919 Antonov-Ovseenko, commander of the RSFSR's Ukrainian Front, disarming the villages, ordered the armies: "beforehand, to take hostages from the kulak elements"[20]. On 29 June 1919 the central organ of the colonial administration of Ukraine (the VUTsIK) legalized "the taking of hostages" by its resolution on general mobilization[21], and on 11 December 1919 Trotsky, war minister of the RSFSR, included "the hostage system" in the list of means by which the armies "entering Ukraine" were obliged to disarm the population[22].
In Zhytomyr, during the nine days of Soviet rule in March 1919, hostages sat in the secret police "until payment of the contribution" - until the town paid the sum imposed on it; so recorded Livshits, commissioner of the Central Committee for Aid to Pogrom Victims, noting that a considerable part of those arrested "as hostages" were Jews[23]. So it was in Fastiv too: until the contribution imposed on the town was paid, Jews sat as hostages[24]. The occupation secret police in Kyiv held hostages as well: Rakovsky, head of the Soviet colonial administration of Ukraine, wrote to Lenin on 9 August 1919 that after the proclamation of "red terror" over the Hryhoriv uprising it had shot 30 hostages - former members of the monarchist organization "Union of the Russian People," which staged pogroms, and former tsarist officials; when it prepared to shoot 15 more and Rakovsky with Latsis, chairman of the Ukrainian secret police, checked their biographies, only two turned out to be dangerous enemies[25].
The removal of hostages from Kyiv during the evacuation at the end of August 1919 was described by the eyewitness Kateryna Koval-Medzvedska: "Doctors, engineers, topographers were put on record, and in the last days, under threat of execution, they were carried off by force to Moscow. Besides that, landowners and the bigger merchants were carried off by the hundreds as hostages"[26].

C1177.T0099 →
C1177 The disarmament of Chechnya: a military operation with artillery shelling and bombing of auls (1925)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

To get at imam Nazhmuddin Gotsinsky, who was sheltering in the mountains of Chechnya, the OGPU (the political police, a predecessor of today's FSB) took old men from the villages and held them hostage under the threat of destroying the villages with artillery. Mikoyan, secretary of the North Caucasus krai committee of the party and one of the operation's organizers, described it himself: "to catch him, the Chekists invited to their quarters old men from the villages where he might be hiding and, declaring them hostages, warned them that if they did not say where Gotsinsky was, they would destroy the villages with artillery, and would arrest them and put them on trial. For a whole day they tried to persuade the old men, but all was without result. Then an aeroplane was sent to that district. They were terribly afraid of the aeroplane. Only after several shells had been fired at the village so that, without destruction of the houses, the inhabitants should feel its power, did the old men say in which gorge Gotsinsky was hiding"[27]. The word "hostages" is also in the documents of the operation itself: on 4 September 1925 Mironov, head of the Chechen oblast department of the OGPU, reported: "I report: the population is giving up Nazhmudin. I am now conducting negotiations with the representatives. The matter is being conducted through the hostages I have released... Let aeroplanes appear at 15 hours, but not strike. For effect let some 6-7 of them come"[28]. For the Sharoy district the count of hostages is known from the documents of the military archive: historian A. Yu. Lashkov writes that "the command of the military grouping demanded of the population of the Sharoy district the surrender of the well-known bandit Gotsinsky. In doing so, 40 men from among the honored elders were taken hostage. However, the demand to surrender Gotsinsky was not satisfied within the appointed time, and therefore the troops resorted to intensified repressions. In two days 22 puds of bombs [about 360 kilograms - like 22 rounds of a modern 120 mm mortar] were dropped on the district"[29]. The surviving companion of Gotsinsky names a different number and adds what is not in the Soviet papers: from the destroyed aul of Khakmedy "105 hostages were taken", and the hostages were threatened with shooting - his account says: "the Bolsheviks wrote of the shooting of the hostages in the event of resistance in their proclamations"[30]. The word "hostages" is not in the disarmament instruction of 4 August 1925, approved by the operation's commanders, - it allows only arrests of "influential persons of the aul" as an extreme measure: the hostage-taking was done over and above what was written, and its traces remained in the dispatches of the executors and the memoirs of an organizer.

In the autumn of 1994, Russia attacked the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria under the name of a puppet opposition, and on 11 December 1994 the Russian army openly invaded the republic. The occupiers took peaceful Chechens hostage and released them in exchange for captured and killed Russian servicemen, weapons or money.
Russian servicemen drew up lists of detained Chechens for exchange and put on them people who by their age could not have carried weapons[33]. According to a certificate by the prison service of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs, from 11 December 1994 to 22 July 1995 the ministry handed over 141 prisoners of the filtration camps for exchange for captured Russian servicemen (see: "Filtration Camp System")[34].
Russian police officers and soldiers promised to release a detained Chechen if his relatives brought money, a Russian soldier out of captivity or the body of a dead one[34][33]. The occupiers used the hostages' relatives to find out where the Armed Forces of Ichkeria were holding captured Russians: the relatives searched for captives to rescue their own, while the Russian FSB collected information from them and on 29 July 1995 drew up a certificate on the places where Russian soldiers were being held[34][35].
On 9 January 1995, Russian servicemen detained Shamsu Magomadov in Grozny (see: "Abduction of People"). In the winter of 1995-1996, his sister Madina Magomadova sought her brother's exchange from Colonel Pilyugin of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs. She told "Memorial": "They told me: 'Yes, your brother is alive, we will exchange him — find a soldier [a captured Russian soldier]'. I found a soldier — I went through all those mountains, villages, there is a war on, under fire I searched for a soldier — I found one, brought a photograph. They tell me: 'The soldier is a deserter, he is not subject to exchange…' … 'Go find an officer'. … I found an officer, brought a photograph of the officer... They tell me: sorry, Comrade Magomadova, your brother has been taken away from here, and where he is supposed to be, he has not been brought yet. We cannot find him". The occupiers never released Shamsu Magomadov, and nothing is known of his fate[34].
Russian servicemen and security services put up for exchange the captured relatives and guards of the president of Ichkeria. On 19 February 1995, the occupiers detained in Grozny Makharbi Dudayev, the president's younger brother, and on 6 April the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs put him up for exchange in Malye Atagi, a village south of Grozny, together with 16 prisoners of the filtration camps. The ministry exchanged the prisoners for a captured Russian major and released the president's brother separately[34][36][37]. On the night of 28 May 1996, Russian border guards detained the widow of the president of Ichkeria with her bodyguard at the airport of Nalchik, the capital of Russian-controlled Kabardino-Balkaria, and the Russian FSB released the widow but exchanged the bodyguard for a captured Russian priest[38][39].
Russian soldiers and filtration camp guards demanded ransom for detained Chechens. In late March 1995, the guards of the military unit near Assinovskaya, a stanitsa in the west of Ichkeria, asked detained Chechens who would pay more to get out[34]. In April 1995, the guards of the filtration camp on the northeastern outskirts of Grozny received five million rubles for the release of a detained Chechen[40]. On 15 May 1995, Russian soldiers put a detained Chechen from Shali, a town southeast of Grozny, into a pit at their unit, offered to let him out for money and released him after 18 hours[41]. In December 1995, Russian servicemen detained five elderly Chechens at a checkpoint, and their relatives ransomed them from a filtration camp for ten million rubles[40]. On 17 April 1996, Russian soldiers detained a Chechen woman with her two brothers at the entrance to Grozny and demanded three million rubles for them, and in the evening released all three, and the brother gave them the money[33]. On 18 April, Russian servicemen took six Chechens from their homes in Novye Aldy, a settlement on the southwestern outskirts of Grozny, and their relatives ransomed them from the filtration camp at the city motor depot[33].
The Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs and the command of the occupation troops demanded captured Russian servicemen, their bodies and weapons for detained Chechens. In March 1996, officers of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs demanded that the female relatives of a Chechen, held since January in the filtration camp in Grozny, dig up and bring the bodies of three officers of a Russian police special unit, and in late March, having received the bodies, released him[34]. In March 1996, soldiers of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs detained three Chechens at the checkpoint between Goity and Starye Atagi, villages south of Grozny, and on 28 March the occupiers exchanged them for five Russian soldiers[42]. On 7 April 1996, Russian servicemen took Saidash Shamakhurov from Gikalovo, a village south of Grozny, to Khankala, the main base of the occupation troops on the eastern outskirts of Grozny, where investigators demanded that he record on tape his request to be exchanged for 15 captured Russian soldiers and 48 construction workers. On 27 April, the occupiers exchanged him for a Russian soldier[42]. In May 1996, soldiers of the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs (predecessors of today's Rosgvardia) detained in the center of Grozny 17 to 20 Chechens who were repairing heating pipes and demanded during interrogation that their relatives bring weapons or captured Russian servicemen for them. The next evening the guards let them go[42].
Russian soldiers took hostages during assaults and when encircled. On 7 April 1995, during the storming of Samashki, a village in the west of Ichkeria, the occupiers took four Chechen men hostage, drove them into a pit in a garage and fired at them with assault rifles. One the occupiers killed on the spot, the wounded second one the next day; two survived (see: "Mass Killings of Civilians")[34]. On 10-12 August 1996, soldiers of the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs, encircled by the Armed Forces of Ichkeria, held about 500 patients, their relatives and medics hostage in City Hospital No. 9 in Grozny. On 12 August, the occupiers left the hospital, taking a hundred hostages as a human shield, and released them near the former driving school where Russian units were stationed, once the defenders had handed over four captured Russian soldiers (see: "Use of People as "Human Shields"")[36][34].
On 11 and 12 August 1996, when the Armed Forces of Ichkeria were liberating Grozny from the occupiers, soldiers of the 101st Brigade of the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs took Chechen men from the blocks around the "15th Gorodok", their base on Lenin Street, and held them there as hostages. On 14 October 1996, Magomed Taramov told "Memorial" how on 12 August Russian servicemen took him from his home together with his son Alimkhan: "We are no 'boyeviki' [as Russians called the Armed Forces of Ichkeria], we took no part in the armed actions. They led us out into the street, and there by the wall stood another nine or so people... In all, about twenty-four of us were gathered". For the release of the hostages, the brigade's officers demanded that relatives bring wounded soldiers from the encircled Russian checkpoints and the bodies of the dead, or get the army of Ichkeria to let food through to those posts. Garib Asiyalov, whom the hostages' female relatives called to the talks at the "15th Gorodok", in October told "Memorial" the words of a Russian officer: "…I answer to Kulikov [the head of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs]. We will trade head for head — our wounded or dead one for your living one". According to him, for the wounded and dead soldiers whom the relatives brought by truck, the brigade released 32 hostages[36]. On 16 August, when relatives brought the bodies of eight Russian servicemen to the base, the occupiers released Magomed and Alimkhan Taramov[34].
On 17 August 1996, soldiers of the 101st Brigade searched the blocks near the "15th Gorodok" and took Chechen men from there to the base as hostages. The brigade's officers promised to release them for the bodies of Russian soldiers buried near the bus station and the chemical plant, and on 19 August, when Russian soldiers brought 28 bodies out through the positions of the Armed Forces of Ichkeria, they released the hostages. The human rights defenders of "Memorial" published the names of twelve of them[36] and write that at the "15th Gorodok" and in City Hospital No. 9 in Grozny the soldiers of the Internal Troops took hostages on the direct orders of the commanders of their units[34].
In his report of 26 March 1996, the UN Secretary-General cited reports by human rights organizations: Russian troops often detained any Chechen man, whether there was evidence against him or not, in order to exchange him for captured Russian soldiers[43]. The human rights defenders of "Memorial" write that Russian agencies repeatedly used civilian prisoners of the filtration camps as hostages and in most cases offered Ichkeria in exchange Chechens who had not taken part in the fighting. In "Memorial's" conclusion, hostage-taking became a regular practice for the Russian military[34].
Russian prosecutors put none of the occupiers who took hostages in Hospital No. 9 and at the "15th Gorodok" in Grozny on trial (see: "Impunity of Perpetrators")[42][34].
In August 1996, the Armed Forces of Ichkeria liberated Grozny from the occupiers, forcing Russia to the negotiating table[44]. On 31 August 1996, peace agreements were signed in Khasavyurt (see: "Sham Treaties")[45]. On 31 December 1996, the last Russian troops left the republic[34].
