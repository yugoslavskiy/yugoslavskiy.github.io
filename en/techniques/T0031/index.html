Restriction of Sovereignty

T0031 Persistence Nokhchi (Chechens), Ukrainians

Aggressors may impose terms that radically curtail the political rights and independence of a local government. Through a categorical ban on conducting an independent foreign policy, depriving an Indigenous people of the right to freely elect their leaders, and direct interference in domestic legislation, the aggressor methodically transforms a self-governing entity into a fully controlled, rightless province.

Procedures Carried Out11

1654 Pereiaslav Council Ukrainians

Pereiaslav Council (1654) Tsardom of Muscovy Diplomacy

From the very beginning, Moscow laid an asymmetric foundation for the relationship, harshly cutting off the autonomy's international agency. Tsar Aleksei's charter (27 March 1654) directly restricted the hetman's foreign relations: "And with the Turkish sultan and the Polish king, without our tsarist majesty's decree, no relations are to be maintained"[1].

1659 Pereiaslav Articles Ukrainians

Pereiaslav Articles (1659) Tsardom of Muscovy Diplomacy

Taking advantage of the political crisis and having surrounded the autonomy's leaders with its troops, the tsardom forced them to sign a new, utterly enslaving treaty. The articles turned the confederative bond with Moscow into a federative one: "the Pereiaslav Articles converted Ukraine's confederative ties with Russia into the form of a federation. The Ukrainian state lost its independence and acquired the status of a political autonomy within the Russian state"[2]. The very basis of the treaty was forged: "Prince Trubetskoy took advantage of his military superiority over Yu. Khmelnytsky, rejected the Zherdiv Articles and forced the Ukrainian side to accept 14 points of the so-called old articles of 1654 (which in reality constituted a falsification of the 1654 treaty)"[2]. The 14 imposed points themselves are published in the "Collection of State Charters and Treaties"[3].

1665 The Moscow Articles of Ivan Briukhovetsky Ukrainians

The Moscow Articles of Ivan Briukhovetsky (1665) Tsardom of Muscovy Diplomacy

Depriving the autonomy of control over its own administrative and economic life: "direct control over the military-administrative and financial-economic life of Ukraine passed into the hands of the tsarist voivodes"[4] – the sovereign functions of the hetman's authority passed to representatives of the metropole.

Procedure page →C0024.T0031

1672 The Konotop Articles of Ivan Samoilovych Ukrainians

The Konotop Articles of Ivan Samoilovych (1672) Tsardom of Muscovy Diplomacy

Interference in internal governance: "the hetman was deprived of the right to arbitrarily (without a hearing before the military court) issue orders for the execution or dismissal of his subordinates"[5] – personnel and judicial decisions were removed from the hetman's sole authority. The norm itself is older than this treaty: it was first fixed by the Konotop Articles of 1672 ("executed and removed from office… by the judgment and proof of the Host's court")[3].

Procedure page →C0025.T0031

1687 Kolomak Articles Ukrainians

Kolomak Articles (1687) Tsardom of Muscovy Diplomacy

The systematic dismantling of the autonomy's democratic institutions. Moscow categorically forbade the Cossacks to elect their leaders independently and to conduct an independent foreign policy: "and without a petition and without a decree of the great sovereigns... a hetman shall not be elected... nor shall they, on their own... write anything to anyone"[6]. The abolition of the historical right to grant asylum: the autonomy was forced to perform the functions of the Muscovite punitive apparatus and to hand over fugitives: "and those fugitives and people of all ranks shall not be received, nor kept among them"[6].

1700–1708 Integration Reforms of Peter I and the Great Northern War Ukrainians

Integration Reforms of Peter I and the Great Northern War (1700–1708) Tsardom of Muscovy Government

Preparation for dismantling the autonomy during the reforms of 1707: by Orlyk's account, the Cossack starshyna expected the empire to be preparing "to turn the Cossacks into a regular army… to break the rights and liberties of the Host"[6][7].

1709 The Reshetylivka Articles Ukrainians

The Reshetylivka Articles (1709) Tsardom of Muscovy Government

Entrenchment of tsarist control over the autonomy's foreign policy: the articles "fully preserved the previously established control of the tsarist regime over… foreign policy"[8].

1728 The Decisive Points Ukrainians

The Decisive Points (1728) Russian Empire Government

Complete diplomatic isolation of the autonomy: "Article 19 forbids the hetman relations with other countries. He was to acquaint the tsar's resident with letters received from abroad, forward their translations to the tsar's court, and meanwhile detain the envoys"[8].

1919–1921 Institutional Absorption through a "Military-Political Union" Ukrainians

Institutional Absorption through a "Military-Political Union" (1919–1921) Soviet Russia (RSFSR) Diplomacy

Transfer of control over the military affairs, finances, communications, and transport routes of the Ukrainian republic into the hands of the central bodies of the RSFSR[9].

Procedure page →C0075.T0031

1922–1924 Legal Absorption through the Creation of the USSR Ukrainians

Legal Absorption through the Creation of the USSR (1922–1924) USSR Government

Stripping the Ukrainian SSR of control over foreign policy, the army, foreign trade, transport, and communications through the 1924 Constitution of the USSR, transferring them to the jurisdiction of the all-union people's commissariats[9].

Procedure page →C0076.T0031

1991 Non-Recognition and the Attempted Military Invasion: Moscow's Response to the Independence of the Chechen Republic Nokhchi (Chechens)

Non-Recognition and the Attempted Military Invasion: Moscow's Response to the Independence of the Chechen Republic (September-November 1991) Russian Federation Government

On 10 September 1991 the RSFSR president Yeltsin sent to Chechen-Ingushetia a delegation headed by state secretary Burbulis and instructed it "to explain the policy of the Russian Federation and of the President of the RSFSR at the present stage"[10]; the delegation came back with nothing[11]. On 8 October the Moscow leadership, over Khasbulatov's signature, resolved "until the election of a new composition of the Supreme Soviet of the Chechen-Ingush Republic, to regard the Provisional Supreme Council of the Republic as the only lawful body of state power on the territory of the Chechen-Ingush Republic" and ordered "the illegal armed formations [this is what the coloniser called the fighters for independence from Russia] to surrender their weapons to the bodies of internal affairs by 24:00 on 10 October 1991"[12]. The Chechens did not surrender their weapons: on 9 October the leadership of the National Congress of the Chechen People replied that the resolution had been adopted "with the sole aim of preserving colonial domination over the Chechen people, over the peoples of the Caucasus and their natural riches", announced the mobilisation of men from 15 to 55 and put the National Guard on combat alert[13]. On 10 October Moscow instructed vice-president Rutskoi and the RSFSR government "to ensure the necessary conditions for the restoration of legality and law and order in the Chechen-Ingush Republic"[14]. On 15 October the chairman of the KGB of the RSFSR (predecessor of today's FSB) Ivanenko suspended the work of the KGB of Chechen-Ingushetia "until 27 October 1991" and by his own order appointed an acting chairman for it[11]. On 19 October Yeltsin addressed the leadership of the congress with an ultimatum: "I demand from the leaders of the Executive Committee of the National Congress of the Chechen People and the groupings supporting them the cessation of unlawful actions and unconditional submission to the law… all measures provided for by the laws of the RSFSR will be taken"[15]. On 24 October Moscow resolved that the elections set for 27 October "have no legal basis and their results cannot be recognised as lawful", and entrusted the preparation of different elections to the Provisional Supreme Council and to the RSFSR deputies from the republic[16]. The Chechens voted anyway: on 27 October, according to the electoral commission of the Chechen Republic, 72 % of voters turned out, and 90.1 % voted for Dzhokhar Dudaev[11]. On 1 November Dzhokhar Dudaev signed a decree: "Guided by the Declaration on the state sovereignty of the republic and by the will of the citizens of the Chechen Republic expressed in direct and universal elections, to proclaim the state sovereignty of the Chechen Republic from 1 November 1991"[17]. On 2 November Moscow answered with a resolution: "To recognise the elections to the supreme body of state power (the Supreme Soviet) and of the President of the republic held in the Chechen-Ingush Republic on 27 October 1991 as unlawful, and the acts adopted by them as not subject to execution"[18]. On 7 November Yeltsin, in decree No. 178, named as the reason for the state of emergency an aggravation "caused by the unlawful actions of the executive committee of the National Congress of the Chechen People and its leaders, who seek, by organising mass disorders, with the use of violence, to remove the lawfully acting state bodies from power", and in point 6 banned any elections: "elections of bodies of state power and of local self-government shall not be held during the whole period of the state of emergency"[19]. None of these bans worked: the elections took place, the president and the parliament of Chechnya were at work, and on 11 November the deputies in Moscow refused to approve the decree itself[20].
