Abduction of People

Aggressors may carry out covert or overt violent abductions of political leaders, civic activists, journalists, and ordinary members of the Indigenous people. In the active phase of a conflict, this technique serves the operational neutralization of defenses by physically eliminating potential organizers of resistance. In the long term, enforced disappearances are used to consolidate the occupation regime, as they create an atmosphere of unpredictable terror and paralyze the society's will to mount systematic protest.

July 1819
C1143.T0053 →
C1143 Demonstrative destruction of the aul of Dadi-Yurt and the construction of the Vnezapnaya fortress to force the Nokhchi off the Kumyk plain (1819)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

The Russian Empire led the Nokhchi who survived the destruction of an aul away as captives into its own territory. General Yermolov himself writes in his "Notes" that women and children "were taken prisoner numbering up to one hundred and forty"[1], and the imperial historian Potto confirms this[2]. Historian D. A. Khozhaev writes that during the crossing of the captives over the Terek, 46 captured girls, "not wishing to endure abuse in captivity," perished, "dragging their guards with them into the turbulent river"[3].

January 1826
C1149.T0053 →
C1149 Destruction of the Refugee Aul of Uzeni-Yurt, Extortion of Hostages from Geldigen, and Capture of Samashki Residents at Harvest (1826-1827)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

During the destruction of Uzeni-Yurt on January 10, 1827, Cossacks of the detachment of General Laptev, commander of the left flank of the Caucasian Line, took captive three women from among the fleeing inhabitants. The imperial historian Potto recorded: the Cossacks "seized... three women"[4]. In the summer of 1827, having encircled the population of the aul of Samashki in an ambush during the harvest, the Cossacks of General Engelhardt, commander of the left flank of the Caucasian Line, took captive the families of resistance leaders: the leader Tara-Adzuev and the family of Bakar-Bulatov; Bulatov himself managed to break through the cordon. Potto wrote: "Tara-Adzuev and Bulatov's family they carried off with them"[4].

January 1826
C1148.T0053 →
C1148 Devastation of the Lowland Nokhchi Villages in Yermolov’s Punitive Campaign (1826)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

Historian D. A. Khozhaev writes that by May 18, 1826, «women and children had been taken captive» from the Nokhchi[3]. General Yermolov himself, in a report to Emperor Nicholas I dated May 28, 1826, specified the number: in the surprise attack on the village of Stavna-kul on May 2, «16 souls of both sexes were taken captive», and in the attack on Malaya Ataga on May 16 he «captured 15 souls of both sexes»[5]. The imperial historian Volkonsky confirms the seizure at Stavnokol: the sudden movement «brought us sixteen captives»[6].

September 1830
C1152.T0053 →
C1152 Burning of Lowland Chechnya by Velyaminov's Expedition: Demolition of Villages from the Sunzha to Mairtup, Seizure of Astemir's Family, Collective Penalties Imposed on Chechen Villages, the Cutting Down of Fleeing Inhabitants of Dzulgai-Yurt, and Destruction of Winter Stores (1830-1831)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

At dawn on December 19, 1830, during the seizure of the village of Dzulgai-Yurt, Cossacks of General Velyaminov's detachment captured the family of a resistance leader — the Karabulak chieftain Astemir, who himself managed to escape into the forest. The imperial historian Potto wrote: "his family — a young son, a daughter, a grandson, and a cousin — fell into the hands of the Cossacks. Besides them, thirty-nine people were taken prisoner"[4].

December 1831
C1154.T0053 →
C1154 Velyaminov's winter raids on Chechen hamlets and villages along the Sunzha: capture of women and devastation of homesteads (1831-1832)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

On December 23, 1831, during the devastation of the hamlets near the village of Chertugai, Cossacks of the detachment of Lieutenant Colonel Zass, commander of the Mozdok Cossack Regiment, took three women captive. The imperial historian Volkonsky wrote: Zass "delivered three captive women to the camp"[6].

August 1832
C1155.T0053 →
C1155 Extermination of 61 settlements of lowland Chechnya and mountainous Ichkeria by Rosen's troops, burning alive of the defenders of Germenchuk, and extortion of hostages from 80 villages (1832)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

On September 9, 1832, during the seizure of the village of Tsentoroy in Ichkeria, the column of General Volkhovsky from the detachment of corps commander Rosen took captive a woman from among the residents who had not managed to flee. The imperial historian Volkonsky wrote: "one woman was taken prisoner"[6]. On September 15, near the village of Dungen-yurt, the cavalry of Major Boreysha took captive residents caught while attempting to leave their homes. Rosen reported to Minister of War Chernyshev: the cavalry "managed to take prisoner 4 persons and 1 woman"[7].

January 1835
C1156.T0053 →
C1156 Covert reconnaissance of Chechnya by Rosen's topographers and the devastation of Zandak by Pullo's detachment: capture of 31 inhabitants and seizure of livestock (1835-1836)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

On August 23, 1836, during the sudden seizure of the aul of Zandak, the detachment of Colonel Pullo, chief of the Sunzha fortified line, took captive residents who had not managed to escape through the ravines and the forest. Corps commander Rosen reported to the Minister of War: the infantry drove out the defenders, "having taken prisoner 31 souls of male and female sex"[7].

December 1836
C1158.T0053 →
C1158 Extermination of the Inhabitants of Kishkeroy-Yurt by Pullo's Detachment: 76 Killed, 40 Captured, Village Burned with Its Stores (December 1836)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

In the course of the extermination of Kishkeroy-Yurt on 28-31 December 1836, Pullo's detachment led away 40 inhabitants into captivity: "40 souls of both sexes were taken captive"[8].

January 1837
C1160.T0053 →
C1160 Annihilation of Lesser Chechnya by Feze's Expedition: 92 Captives, More Than a Thousand Saklias Burned (January 1837)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

During the annihilation of Lesser Chechnya by Feze's expedition on 23-29 January 1837, the troops led 92 inhabitants away into captivity. The expedition journal, presented by Rozen to Minister of War Chernyshev on 11 February 1837, counts by episode: at the taking of Shalazh-yurt "67 souls were taken captive", at Urus-Martan "13 souls were captured", at Khankharik-Roshnya and Birzul the detachment "took 12 souls captive"[7].

February 1837
C1161.T0053 →
C1161 Coercion of Chechnya into Oaths by Feze's Spring Tour: Amanats from Communities Coerced into the Oath, Destruction of Auls along the Dzhalka, a Livestock Fine (February-April 1837)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

Over Feze's spring tour across Chechnya (February-April 1837), the troops led away dozens of inhabitants into captivity, most of them unarmed. The summary in Rozen's report to Minister of War Chernyshev of 4 March 1837: for 12-22 February "52 souls of inhabitants who did not submit voluntarily were taken captive by our troops, including 8 armed men"[7] - that is, 44 of the 52 captives bore no arms. The report of 23 February on the burning of Seid-yurt and Avtur: "one Chechen and three women who had not managed to get away into the forest" were taken[7]. On the night of 22 March at Gurdali, according to the report of 12 April, Lieutenant Colonel Panteleyev "took captive 9 unsubmissive Chechens"; in the April raid on the Gertli settlement "18 more souls belonging to the families of the brigands were seized"[7].

November 1838
C1164.T0053 →
C1164 Forcing Chechnya into submission after the fall of Akhulgo and suppression of the general uprising of 1840 (1838-1840)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

At the destruction of the aul of Mirti-Irzau on 4-5 November 1840 (an aul on the Sunzha where the inhabitants of Zakan-Yurt and Samashki had resettled) the troops under general Grabbe, commander of the troops of the Caucasus Line (the chain of imperial fortifications on the land of the Nokhchi), led away Chechen families into captivity. The imperial historian Yurov recorded: "We took captive several families of the Terek-side Chechens, numbering 35 souls, and seized 150 head of horned cattle" (Terek-side - the Chechens of the left bank of the Terek)[9]. The captivity of families stands in the imperial account in one line with the driven-off cattle.

January 1847
C1166.T0053 →
C1166 "Death sentence" for Lesser Chechnya: the massacre of Duba's hamlet, forest clearings, fortresses, annihilation of the population and stanitsas on the sites of burned auls (1847-1851)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

Every strike on the auls of Lesser Chechnya (the lowland-piedmont western part of Chechnya) ended with people being carried off. From the massacre of the hamlet of naib Duba (a deputy of Imam Shamil, leader of the highlanders' resistance to the Russian conquest) on 6 March 1847 the detachment, by the record of imperial officer Poltoratsky, "dragged along behind it 9 captives: three toothless old men and six women"[10]. In the raid of 17 February 1850, "54 souls of both sexes" were taken captive[11]; at the crossing of the Sunzha river on 15 September 1851 - 38 captives[11]; at the annihilation of Dakhin-Irzau on 16 November 1851, "24 souls were taken captive"[11]. The count in "souls of both sexes" in the reports is a count of civilians carried off, not fighters.

C0091 Stalling Democratic Reforms and Countering the People's Movement (Rukh) (1989–1990)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

The forced disappearance of a key functionary of the movement: "on the night of January 15–16, 1994, two armed unknown men burst into the NRU secretariat in Kyiv, took some documents, and searched for Boichyshyn" - Mykhailo Boichyshyn, head of the Rukh secretariat, was never seen again. The investigation was sabotaged: law enforcement "at first was in no hurry to accept the report of the politician's disappearance, and then demonstratively displayed disinterest in solving the crime," and "the investigators were interested not so much in the circumstances of the attack on the Secretariat and Boichyshyn's disappearance as in the party's financing"[12].

September 1994
C1193.T0053 →
C1193 First Russian-Chechen War (1994–1996)
G0011 Russian Federation
S0010 Regular Army

In the autumn of 1994, Russia attacked the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria under the name of a puppet opposition, and on 11 December 1994 the Russian army openly invaded the republic. The occupiers abducted Chechens and concealed information about their fate, whereabouts or condition from their relatives.

Russian servicemen, fighters of OMON (police special forces) and of the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs (predecessors of today's Rosgvardia) detained Chechens at checkpoints and led them away from their homes, sometimes at night[13][14][15]. Russian soldiers checked passports against arrest lists, which they received from the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs, detained Chechens by them even with documents and passes, and then handed them over to the headquarters of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs in Ichkeria. Women and old people also ended up on these lists[16].

Russian servicemen and police officers did not let detained Chechens inform their families about themselves, and their relatives considered them missing. The occupiers put the abducted in pits at military units and did not register the arrest (see: "Filtration Camp System")[14][15].

The Russian military transported detained Chechens from commandant's offices to filtration camps, and on the way people often disappeared without a trace. When relatives found out where a person was being held, the Russian guards immediately moved him elsewhere[16].

To inquiries about missing people who appeared on the lists of the filtration camps, Russian agencies replied that these people had been released[14]. The Chechen society "Victims of War" searched for Chechens whom the occupiers had detained in Grozny and at checkpoints from 11 December 1994 to May 1995, and did not find a single one of them on the Russian list of 252 released. At the addresses from the Russian lists, the women of the society found no people with such surnames[16].

Relatives of the missing filed statements with the society "Victims of War". As of 8 August 1995, 1308 peaceful citizens of Ichkeria were listed as missing[14]. By mid-October 1996, the society's list held 1432 missing Chechens[17]. In the spring of 1997, Ichkeria handed Russia a list of 1563 missing[14].

The Russian military tortured and killed some of the abducted, and relatives later found their bodies where Russian units had been stationed (see: "Killing of Prisoners and Detainees")[14].

On 9 January 1995, the occupiers detained Shamsa Magomadov in Grozny, and the guards of the filtration camp in Mozdok, a town in Russian-controlled North Ossetia, recorded that they had released him on 2 February[16]. Shamsa Magomadov did not return home, and in the winter of 1995-1996 the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs promised his sister to exchange her brother for a captured Russian soldier (see: "Hostage-Taking")[16][14]. On 21 May 1995, at checkpoint No. 13 near Samashki, a village in the west of Ichkeria, the Russian military detained Ruslan Parshoev from that village, and the Russian major Morozov took him to his unit near Assinovskaya, a stanitsa in the west of Ichkeria. The next day, Lvov, the chief of staff of the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs in Ichkeria, ordered the detainee sent to Khankala, the main base of the occupation troops on the eastern outskirts of Grozny, where he was taken by fighters of the "Rus" special forces unit, and Ruslan Parshoev went missing[14]. In the summer of 1995, the occupiers abducted at a checkpoint on the road to Grozny Khamad Kurbanov, the representative of the president of Ichkeria in Russia, who had just been released after a month without charge in a Moscow prison[18]. Russian guards held Khamad Kurbanov in a pit near Assinovskaya and in the filtration camp in Grozny until a commission under the international OSCE mission secured his release at the negotiations between Russia and Ichkeria[16]. On 23 December 1995, the Russian military, working from lists, took Ruslan Soslambekov, chairman of the movement "People's Diplomacy for Peace and Unity", at a checkpoint at the entrance to Grozny, kept him for 28 days in the filtration camp on the outskirts of the city, then released him[15].

In February 1996, the Russian military stopped at checkpoints 26 acquaintances of Madina Magomadova from the society "Victims of War", and all of them disappeared[16].

At dawn on 1 August 1996, OMON fighters of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs in helicopters, jeeps and armored personnel carriers attacked the settlement of Dolinsky north-west of Grozny[19]. Movla Idrisov from Dolinsky told a Reuters correspondent on the day of the raid that the OMON men went into the first and second floors, led away all the men they found, and left one: a 65-year-old with one leg, on crutches[20]. The urgent action of Amnesty International of 6 August 1996 says that the OMON men led away 18 Chechens, among them Aslanbek Gassayev and Umakh Mogamedov: "The men were led with sacks over their heads to a waiting bus and driven away. They have not been seen since"[19]. In 1996, the UN Working Group on Enforced Disappearances requested from Russia information on the fate of two of them, and in 1997 Russia replied that their cases were being investigated by the Prosecutor General's Office, the Ministry of Internal Affairs and the FSB[21][22].

On 24 August 1996, eight Russian soldiers led away from a yard in Grozny Aleksei Karmayev, a 67-year-old disabled man who had complained to the headquarters of the 276th Regiment of the Russian army about looting, after which the disabled man went missing[17][23].

By the end of October 1996, the prison department of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs replied to a list of more than 1300 missing, handed over after the ceasefire in June 1996, that forty people from the list had been held in custody and released, but all forty had been released before 1993, before the war. About the rest the department said nothing. On 22 January 1997, Izmailov, an officer of the Russian search group, said on air on the ORT television channel that the missing Chechens were most likely no longer alive, and did not specify whether the Russian military had killed them in battle or shot them on detention, or whether they had died in custody[17]. In January 1998, the UN Working Group on Enforced Disappearances reported that Russia had not clarified the fate of a single missing person about whom it had been asked[22].

On 10 June 1996, the working groups of Russia and Ichkeria on the search for the missing agreed to stop detentions based on lists and other papers without legal force[24].

In August 1996, the Armed Forces of Ichkeria liberated Grozny from the occupiers, forcing Russia to the negotiating table[25]. On 31 August 1996, peace agreements were signed in Khasavyurt (see: "Sham Treaties")[26]. On 31 December 1996, the last Russian troops left the republic[14].
