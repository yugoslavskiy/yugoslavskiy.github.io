Collective Punishment

Aggressors may apply collective punishment: for the actions of individuals, reprisals fall on the entire community - villages and districts are subjected to monetary levies, troops are quartered at the residents' expense, families and relatives are exiled, dwellings are demolished, hostages are taken. The prohibition is entrenched in every key codification of the law of war: Article 50 of the Hague Regulations on the Laws and Customs of War on Land (1899, 1907) forbids general penalties imposed on the population for the acts of individuals; Article 33 of the Fourth Geneva Convention (1949) expressly prohibits "collective penalties and likewise all measures of intimidation or of terrorism"; Additional Protocol II to the Geneva Conventions (1977) extends the prohibition to non-international armed conflicts, and the ICRC study of customary international humanitarian law (2005, Rule 103) records it for conflicts of any kind.

September 1830
C1152.T0159 →
C1152 Burning of Lowland Chechnya by Velyaminov's Expedition: Demolition of Villages from the Sunzha to Mairtup, Seizure of Astemir's Family, Collective Penalties Imposed on Chechen Villages, the Cutting Down of Fleeing Inhabitants of Dzulgai-Yurt, and Destruction of Winter Stores (1830-1831)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

In the autumn of 1830, General Velyaminov introduced collective property levies on Chechen villages that bore no responsibility for the actions of others: for the driving off by Chechens of ten head of cattle from the stanitsa of Chervlennaya, he "immediately ordered that ten head of cattle likewise be taken from the inhabitants of the two villages of Braguny and Novy-Yurt, past which the party had traveled, and returned to the Cossacks"; for the driving off of a herd of horses in the Nogai steppe, "Braguny once again paid with exactly the same number of head"; and Shakh-Girei, the owner of Novy-Yurt, was confined to a casemate — a prison cell of the Groznaya fortress — for the inaction of the village watch. This was recorded by the imperial historian Potto[1].

February 1837
C1161.T0159 →
C1161 Coercion of Chechnya into Oaths by Feze's Spring Tour: Amanats from Communities Coerced into the Oath, Destruction of Auls along the Dzhalka, a Livestock Fine (February-April 1837)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

By the April 1837 raid on the Gertli settlement the troops of General Feze imposed a collective property levy on the whole village: according to Rozen's report to Minister of War Chernyshev of 30 May, on top of the livestock taken during the raid "a fine of 132 head of livestock was imposed on the settlement"[2] - the settlement paid as a whole, with no regard to the personal involvement of its residents.

October 1905
C1175.T0159 →
C1175 Collective punishment in the abrek era: fines, quartering of troops and exile for Zelimkhan (1905-1913)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0012 Occupation and Controlled Administrations

Following the Chervlyonnaya conferences of 1905-1906 the Russian Empire established a mechanism of circular monetary responsibility: fines on the guilty, "and in the event of their financial insolvency - the penal sanctions passed to the village community to which the guilty party belonged"[3]. Starye Atagi was fined twice for sheltering its fellow villager, an abrek - 3, then 6 thousand rubles; the same village was ordered, within three days, to hand over "100 rifles, two thousand cartridges and 500 daggers", to give up the abreks Tomayev and Khorsiev who were sheltering in the village, and "to collect funds for equipping the village police station with telephone communication"[3]. Tsatsan-Yurt in 1908 paid a "contribution" for undelivered weapons - 7 thousand rubles[3]. In 1911, by order of the Viceroy, "100 thousand rubles were exacted" from the entire Chechen population of the Grozny and Vedeno okrugs in compensation to those who had suffered "...during the attack of Zelimkhan's band"[3].

January 1919
C0074.T0159 →
C0074 Soviet-Ukrainian War: Second Invasion and Resource Depletion (1919)
G0013 Soviet Russia (RSFSR)
S0010 Regular Army

On 29 June 1919 the central organ of the colonial administration of Ukraine (the VUTsIK) resolved: "To take the most energetic measures to suppress kulak uprisings and the actions of bands, applying the imposition of contributions on rebellious localities and the taking of hostages"; the resolution was signed for the chairman of that organ by Polozov and by its secretary Lapchinsky[4]. Under this resolution it was not the one who rose who had to answer - in money and in hostages - but the whole locality at once.

By the end of the year Moscow fixed the practice for the entire army: on 11 December 1919 Trotsky, war minister of the RSFSR, and Kamenev, commander-in-chief of the RSFSR armed forces, signed secret order No. 579 - every army "undertakes, in its area of operations, to disarm the population with the help of all means at its disposal (agitation, agent intelligence, payment for surrendered weapons in money and in kind, house-to-house searches, fines in money and in kind, collective responsibility, the hostage system, the shooting of culprits, etc., etc.)," and the order, as stated in it, applies "first of all to the armies entering Ukraine"[5].

On 26 July 1919 Skrypnyk, plenipotentiary of the same administration's Defence Council, telegraphed from the area of Zeleny's uprising - in the same words as the orders: "The uprising of the peasants of the Trypillia district has been suppressed... there now remains... the exaction from it of all the expenses borne by the workers' and peasants' state for the suppression," specifying: "It is necessary to carry out the exaction in produce, the exaction from the peasants of the expenses caused by the uprising"[6]. In Mohyliv-Podilskyi, which Soviet troops captured on 5 April 1919 without a fight, the town was at once subjected to a forced payment - a telegram to the Bureau of the Ukrainian Press over the signature of Ivanitsky reported: "there is exemplary order in the town, with which even the bourgeoisie is content, despite the imposed contribution of one and a half million"[7]. The reports of the Special Investigative Commission attached to the Whites' command (the AFSR) record of Odesa: "400 people have been sent to forced labour for non-payment of contributions," and of Izium in Kharkiv province: "eyewitnesses told of the wholesale robbery of families whose relatives had gone over to the 'Kadets'"[8].

In Fastiv in Kyiv province the forced payment imposed on the town under the Soviet authority was paid by the Jewish community - Kh. Hofman, an officer of the Editorial Board, the body collecting testimony on the pogroms, recorded: "The entire imposed contribution was paid almost wholly by the Jews, notwithstanding that they are poorer and considerably fewer than the Christians. Many Jews were taken hostage, sat long in prison, many had to leave Fastiv altogether"[9].

July 1925
C1177.T0159 →
C1177 The disarmament of Chechnya: a military operation with artillery shelling and bombing of auls (1925)
G0010 USSR
S0010 Regular Army

Even before the operation the village assemblies together with the revkoms (unelected committees installed in place of elected Soviets - campaign C1176) drew up lists of the "vicious element" - inhabitants noted in robberies; historian E. F. Zhupikova writes: "They and their relatives bore material responsibility for all cases of theft on the territory of Chechnya"[10] - the relatives paid for others' thefts by the fact of kinship and an entry in the list. One could leave the list only by an oath with an obligation to inform: "If the 'vicious element' took an oath that for a year he would not rob and would inform on thieves, he was struck from the list"[10]. When the troops reached the mountains, the destroyed was counted by the same category: as the result of the bombing of 16 auls "119 houses of the 'vicious element' were blown up"[10]; historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov confirm the number - "119 houses were blown up"[3]: a house was blown up because its owner was entered in the list.

September 1994
C1193.T0159 →
C1193 First Russian-Chechen War (1994–1996)
G0011 Russian Federation
S0010 Regular Army

In the autumn of 1994, Russia attacked the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria under the name of a puppet opposition, and on 11 December 1994 the Russian army openly invaded the republic. The occupiers shelled and bombed entire villages and towns of Ichkeria for refusing to submit.

As a rule, the command of the occupation troops demanded that the Chechens in the villages hand over their weapons, not fire at Russian soldiers, or make the Armed Forces of Ichkeria leave the village[11]. In other villages Russian commanders demanded the handover of captured Russian servicemen or passage for their vehicles through the village[12][13]. Commanders of the occupation troops, citing their intelligence data, demanded that the Chechens in a village hand over an arbitrary number of assault rifles in exchange for safety[14].

If a village did not meet the demands, Russian troops surrounded it. The occupiers did not let Chechens out of the village and did not let food and medicine through to them (see: "Medical and Humanitarian Blockade"). Russian artillery shelled the surrounded village, and aircraft bombed it (see: "Indiscriminate Attacks"). The Russian military opened fire before the deadline they themselves had set, or after the village had handed over its weapons[11][14].

After fighters of the army of Ichkeria took Russian special forces soldiers prisoner on 8 January 1995, Russian pilots in helicopters threatened to bomb Komsomolskoye, Alkhazurovo and Roshni-Chu, villages to the southwest of Grozny, and then struck the outskirts of Roshni-Chu, although the special forces soldiers were not in the village[11][12]. In January the crews of Russian helicopters scattered over Bamut, a village to the southwest of Grozny, leaflets of the command of the occupation troops threatening to respond with "powerful missile and bomb strikes" if fire was opened from the village at Russian troops[11]. In January the occupiers scattered over Shali, a town to the southeast of Grozny, leaflets threatening to open "return fire" "on the whole village" for "a single shot [toward the occupiers] from Shali"[11][15].

From 26 February to 3 March 1995 the Russian military at the post near Samashki, a village in the west of Ichkeria, threatened the elders with a strike on the village if they did not persuade their fellow villagers to "settle the matter peacefully"[15]. On 8 March the elders undertook to get the army of Ichkeria out of the village and hand over heavy weapons, and the Russian general Kosolapov signed an agreement with them under which Russian troops would not enter Samashki and would not use force against Chechen civilians (see: "False Guarantees of Safety"). The elders fulfilled their obligation, but from 13 March the Russian military at the posts let no one out of Samashki for 25 days and did not let food and medicine through (see: "Transport Blockade")[15]. On 6 April Romanov, the deputy commander of the occupation troops, demanded that by seven in the morning of 7 April the village hand over 264 assault rifles, two machine guns and one infantry fighting vehicle and let the Internal Troops of the Russian MVD (predecessors of today's Rosgvardia) into the village, but the elders replied that there were not that many weapons in the village[11][15]. On 7 April a Russian general who refused to give his name gave the elders until 4 p.m., but the occupiers began shelling the village with mortars earlier. Yuzbek Shovkhalov, an elder of Samashki who took part in the talks with the command of the occupation troops, told a Memorial observer on 9 April 1995 what happened when Russian troops stormed the village: "'Give us the militants [this is what the Russians called the Armed Forces of Ichkeria].' I say: 'There are no militants here'… Armored personnel carriers... They fire from heavy machine guns. Right in the village. At the house where people live... Either they burn the house down, or the house perishes, anything at all..."[15]. On 7 and 8 April the Russian military killed 103 Chechen civilians in Samashki, whom human rights defenders recorded by name (see: "Mass Killings of Civilians")[15][11].

On 3 March 1995 the command of the occupation troops demanded that the Chechens in Argun, Gudermes and Shali, towns to the east and southeast of Grozny, persuade the Armed Forces of Ichkeria to leave in order to save people's lives[11][15]. On 23 March the occupiers captured Argun, and on 30-31 March, when the defenders were forced to leave, Gudermes and Shali (see: "Military Intervention")[11]. On 8 April Labunets, the commander of the occupation troops in the south of Ichkeria, threatened in writing to shell Zakan-Yurt, a village to the east of Samashki, with artillery if the Chechens in the village did not hand over their weapons and surrender the fighters of the army of Ichkeria, and on 11 April soldiers of the Russian MVD searched every yard and found no weapons[16][15]. In April 1995 the Chechens in Bachi-Yurt, Koshkeldy and Alleroy, villages in the east of Ichkeria, under threat of bombing bought assault rifles and handed them over to the occupiers, but the Russian military shelled these villages anyway (see: "Total Destruction of Infrastructure")[14][11]. The Internal Troops of the Russian MVD and the Russian police kept Sernovodsk, a village in the west of Ichkeria where more than 10,000 people and more than 3,000 refugees lived, encircled for more than five months, from 30 September 1995 until the storming in March 1996[13][11]. Artemov, the commander of the occupation troops in the southwest of Ichkeria, threatened the village in writing with artillery and air strikes if the village did not disarm about a hundred fighters of the army of Ichkeria and let Russian vehicles through Sernovodsk[13].

On 11 February 1996 the command of the occupation troops demanded that the Chechens in Novogroznensky, a settlement in the east of Ichkeria, hand over their weapons and expel the defenders, and when the defenders did not leave, the occupiers killed at least 50 Chechen civilians in the settlement by shelling and storming (see: "Indiscriminate Attacks")[11]. In March 1996 the Russian military demanded that Katyr-Yurt, a village to the southwest of Grozny, hand over assault rifles and ten captured Russian soldiers who were not in the village, and on 28 March a Russian pilot dropped bombs on the village and killed nine people from the Khazhgiriyev family (see: "Indiscriminate Attacks")[12][11].

In the spring of 1996 the command of the occupation troops and the puppet government in Grozny forced villages to sign a "Protocol on Peace and Accord". Under this document a village undertook to surrender the defenders, hand over weapons and let the Russian military in, and for a single fighter of the army of Ichkeria found in the village the occupiers threatened to shoot Chechens and destroy the village[12]. On 31 March 1996 Russian President Yeltsin, by Decree No. 435, made the signing of such protocols part of his program for Ichkeria[17], and Russian aircraft bombed villages that refused to sign the protocol[11].

On 17 April 1996 the Chechens in Chiri-Yurt, a village to the south of Grozny, released captured Russian servicemen under an ultimatum from General Shamanov, but as the occupiers' vehicles were leaving the village, Russian tank crews with a single shot razed to the ground the house of the family of Abdulla Dakhayev, the representative of the authorities of Ichkeria in Chiri-Yurt[11]. On 19 April the occupiers surrounded Shali, and on 25 April demanded that the town surrender the defenders, hand over weapons and release captured Russian soldiers. On the same day Russian pilots bombed the town (see: "Indiscriminate Attacks")[18][11]. At the end of April the elders of Komsomolskoye signed a peace agreement with the occupiers, but from 30 April to 1 May Russian pilots bombed the village and wounded four Chechen civilians[19]. On 6 May Russian troops captured Shali without a fight; there turned out to be no defenders or prisoners there[11]. On that day the occupiers surrounded Komsomolskoye and threatened to destroy it if the fighters of the army of Ichkeria did not hand over their weapons by nightfall, but at about three in the afternoon, before the deadline, Russian artillery opened fire and killed several Chechen civilians[12].

On 8 July 1996 Tikhomirov, the commander of the occupation troops, at a press conference in Khankala, the main base of the occupation troops on the eastern outskirts of Grozny, threatened Ichkeria with retaliatory measures if Zelimkhan Yandarbiyev, the acting president of Ichkeria, did not return all Russian prisoners on 9 July[20]. From 9 to 15 July Russian troops surrounded and shelled Gekhi, a village to the southwest of Grozny where a unit of the army of Ichkeria was stationed, and Makhkety, a mountain village in the southeast of the republic. In Gekhi the occupiers killed 19 Chechen civilians, in Makhkety 25 (see: "Indiscriminate Attacks")[21][22][23]. From 10 July the occupation command introduced a curfew in Ichkeria and banned driving into and out of Grozny by car[11][22].

The Russian military destroyed the houses of relatives of the leaders of Ichkeria and the commanders of its army[11][22]. On 15 July 1996 the crews of seven Russian helicopters struck Starye Atagi, a village to the south of Grozny, with missiles and destroyed the house of the mother of Zelimkhan Yandarbiyev, the acting president of Ichkeria, but the mother and the neighbors survived[22][21]. In the summer of 1996 the Russian military blew up the house of the father of Shamil Basayev, one of the commanders of the army of Ichkeria, in the mountain village of Vedeno[11].

On 19 August 1996 Pulikovsky, the acting commander of the occupation troops, demanded that the army of Ichkeria leave Grozny and gave the citizens of Ichkeria 48 hours to leave the city, where, according to some data, at least 50-70 thousand people remained, and according to others, 300 thousand[11][22][24]. The general threatened after that deadline to strike the city with aircraft, artillery and multiple rocket launchers, which cannot fire with precision, and on the night of 20 August, without waiting for the deadline to expire, the occupiers resumed the shelling and bombing of Grozny[22].

The human rights defenders of Memorial write that the command of the occupation troops made its demands of a whole village and, in case of refusal, threatened reprisals against everyone who lived in it[11]. A Human Rights Watch report of May 1995 says that demands to hand over weapons sometimes serve the Russian military as a pretext for attacking a village or for detaining Chechens[14]. It is unknown how many such crimes the occupation troops committed from December 1994 to August 1996.

On 21 August 1996 General Lebed, the secretary of the Russian Security Council, canceled General Pulikovsky's threat to strike Grozny and called it a bad joke to journalists[22][25].

In August 1996, the Armed Forces of Ichkeria liberated Grozny from the occupiers, forcing Russia to the negotiating table[22]. On 31 August 1996, peace agreements were signed in Khasavyurt (see: "Sham Treaties")[26]. On 31 December 1996, the last Russian troops left the republic[11].
