Administrative Corruption and Extortion

T0146 Extraction of Benefits, Persistence Nokhchi (Chechens)

Aggressors may use their military-administrative apparatus and monopoly position in seized or colonized territories to create a system of shadow levies and systematic extortion. Representatives of the occupation administration artificially erect bureaucratic, logistical, and legal barriers, forcing the Indigenous population to pay unregulated bribes to exercise basic rights — to conduct trade, to move freely, or to keep their property. This practice is used for the direct extraction of benefits through the illegal enrichment of the metropole's apparatus at the expense of the local population's resources. At the same time, it serves as an instrument of institutional consolidation of control, as it turns arbitrariness into the norm and demonstrates to the colonized society its absolute lack of rights before the aggressor's officials.

1691–1700 Punitive Raids and the Economic Strangulation of the Nokhchi Nokhchi (Chechens)

Punitive Raids and the Economic Strangulation of the Nokhchi (1691–1700) Tsardom of Muscovy Occupation and Controlled Administrations

The occupation administration of the Tsardom of Muscovy engaged in systemic lawless arbitrariness against the Indigenous population: the historian Sh. B. Akhmadov states that «the venality and bribe-taking of the voivodes, who permitted themselves great abuses — all this was far from a new phenomenon»[1].

1836–1840 The amanat-and-tribute system of colonel Pullo in the seized auls of Chechnya Nokhchi (Chechens)

The amanat-and-tribute system of colonel Pullo in the seized auls of Chechnya (1836-1840) Russian Empire Regular Army

The overseers appointed by the empire over the auls (the pristavs), within the amanat-and-tribute system of Pullo, commander of the left flank of the Caucasus Line (a chain of imperial fortifications on the land of the Nokhchi), systematically robbed the inhabitants of the seized auls of Chechnya under the guise of collections. The imperial historian Yurov described the mechanism - the pristavs lived off exactions, since "not all pristavs received a salary": from the Chechens, "under the pretext of collecting tributes and fines, the best weapons were taken away, and other entirely harmless but valuable articles of everyday use; it happened that people innocent of anything were arrested at the prompting of simple and often ill-intentioned interpreters... and during expeditions forced collections were permitted"[2]. The empire knew and would not listen: the historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write from archival documents that two Chechen elders brought to the Kabardian prince Bekovich-Cherkassky, who served the empire and was influential on the Terek river, "a petition drawn up on behalf of many Chechens, in which they complain of the oppressions and injustices of major-general Pullo" - the prince refused to accept the petition[3]; according to Yurov, the Chechens "had prepared a petition against him, but did not dare to set it in motion for fear that this general would remain their superior as before"[2]. The empire's survey "The Region in Political and Military Respects", sent by the empire's Minister of War Chernyshev to general Neidhardt, who soon headed the empire's troops in the Caucasus, on 2 April 1842, summed it up: "taking advantage of the abuses, the exactions and the indignation arising from the confiscation of weapons, Shamil little by little stirred all Chechnya to revolt" (Shamil - imam, leader of the resistance of the highlanders of Dagestan and Chechnya to the Russian conquest)[4].

1858–1917 "Military-Popular Administration": the Colonial Regime of the Terek Oblast Nokhchi (Chechens)

"Military-Popular Administration": the Colonial Regime of the Terek Oblast (1858-1917) Russian Empire Occupation and Controlled Administrations

Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: fine moneys were spent on maintaining exiles and secret agents, and at times covered officials' embezzlement - in 1893 the head of the Nadterechny uchastok, lieutenant Tomashevsky, embezzled over 300 rubles collected from Chechen villages[3]. The nature of the power was admitted from within by colonel Belik, appointed in 1860 the first head of the Chechen okrug; he later wrote: in Chechnya "there reigns for the most part not law but arbitrary rule, often founded on the dishonesty of the chief, and thereby we only sow in the people distrust of ourselves, even hatred"[3].

1860–1917 Land plunder: the colonial repartition of land, land hunger and Cossack colonization of the Terek oblast Nokhchi (Chechens)

Land plunder: the colonial repartition of land, land hunger and Cossack colonization of the Terek oblast (1860-1917) Russian Empire Occupation and Controlled Administrations

The ban on leasing land directly to the highlanders, introduced at the end of the 19th century, produced middlemen: "The result of this decision was the appearance of a large number of intermediaries through whom the land still reached the highlanders, but at a higher price"[3]. Who profited from it is named by historian Z. Kh. Ibragimova: "Speculative leasing became widespread; it was practiced by officials of the government and administration and by officers, who rented large areas at 10-15 kopecks per desiatina and sublet them to the highland population at a price several times the original"[5]. The difference in price was pocketed by officials and officers of the very administration that had taken the land away.

1949–1965 "Exiled Forever": Life in Special Settlement and Return to an Occupied Homeland Nokhchi (Chechens)

"Exiled Forever": Life in Special Settlement and Return to an Occupied Homeland (1949-1965) USSR Government

Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that officials of the Organizing Committee, which was in charge of the Chechens' return to the republic, "frequently extorted bribes for passes giving the right to resettle immediately from Kazakhstan and Central Asia to the Caucasus"[3]. The price was named by the chief agronomist of the Ministry of Agriculture of Kazakhstan Kh. U. Arsanukayev in a statement to the head of the government of the USSR Bulganin on 27 August 1957: "the distribution of passes was organized with the calculation that the placemen of the members of the Organizing Committee, and they themselves, filled their suitcases handsomely with money - one pass was sold by them for 2 thousand rubles on average," - and he relayed the words of the returnees: "During our deportation even Beria did not inflict such great material damage on our people as the Organizing Committee of the ChIASSR [the Chechen-Ingush republic] has now inflicted on us"[6]. An official of the government of the RSFSR, Belov, replied on 4 October 1957 that the bribes taken for passes had already been examined by the leadership of the Bolshevik party (the "Central Committee of the CPSU"), that on the Organizing Committee members Tongiev and Dukuzov the party's oblast committee "imposed a strict party reprimand," and proposed "to confine ourselves to the measures taken"[6]. No one returned the houses of the deported: "Wild as it may seem, most often the Chechens had to buy back their houses and land from the new owners"[3] - the collective and state farms of the republic bought up the houses of those who left with state credit: by October 1958 they had on their books 10,413 vacant "residential houses... from the departed population of Ossetian, Avar and other nationalities"[6].

1994–1996 First Russian-Chechen War Nokhchi (Chechens)

First Russian-Chechen War (1994–1996) Russian Federation Regular Army

In the autumn of 1994, Russia attacked the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria under the name of a puppet opposition, and on 11 December 1994 the Russian army openly invaded the republic. Russian servicemen and police officers extorted money, gold, cars and other valuables from Chechens.

At checkpoints, Russian police officers and soldiers took money from drivers for passage and took cars away from Chechens. Such posts stood in Grozny at every stop and on the road from Gudermes to Argun, towns east of Grozny[7].

Russian guards of the filtration camps and of the pits at military units demanded money from detained Chechens for their release (see: "Hostage-Taking")[8][9].

Russian troops surrounded villages and took money from Chechens in exchange for not bombing them and for letting the Chechens leave[7][10]. Russian servicemen demanded weapons that the Chechens did not have and took money instead. Often, a month after the payment, a new Russian unit surrounded the village and demanded money again[7].

On 7-8 April 1995, Russian soldiers who were storming Samashki, a village in the west of Ichkeria, brought the men from Vygonnaya Street out of a basement and demanded gold and money from the women in exchange for not taking the men away. The occupiers took the men, stripped and bound with wire, to the pits near Assinovskaya, a neighboring stanitsa, and to the filtration camp in Mozdok, a town in Russian-controlled North Ossetia (see: "Filtration Camp System")[11]. In May, the headquarters of the joint grouping of the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs in Ichkeria, in a "Memo", prohibited soldiers at the posts from taking belongings and food from Chechens passing through[12].

In the summer of 1995, the puppet government in Grozny began paying compensation from the Russian budget to Chechens whose homes the occupiers had destroyed[13][12]. Officials of the puppet government who distributed the payments usually kept 15 to 50% of each family's money for themselves. By the count of the Chechen journalist Musa Muradov, of the 60 thousand families of Grozny who had lost their housing, no more than one and a half thousand received money[13]. In the autumn of 1995, Russian soldiers at the checkpoints near Achkhoy-Martan and Sernovodsk, villages in the west of Ichkeria, took from everyone who entered or left the village 100,000 rubles (23 dollars) at Achkhoy-Martan and 50,000 rubles at Sernovodsk[14].

Before the assault in March 1996, Chechens in Samashki sold cows to buy weapons, hand them over to the Russian servicemen and save their homes. Zinaida Baisayeva, who lived in the village, told the public tribunal of the Russian Glasnost Foundation in Moscow on 22 April 1996: "Approximately 9-10 times we gave money so that they would not bomb us. But they shelled us anyway every night." On the night of 14 March 1996, the occupiers surrounded Samashki. Russian servicemen told the village head and the elders that they would give a "corridor" so that fighters of the Armed Forces of Ichkeria could leave the village at night, and that they would then check passports and leave. Zainap Baisayeva from this village told the Glasnost tribunal: "For this one-hour corridor they asked, as I was told, 58 million rubles. This money was collected from the residents of the village. My brother gave a million [rubles] — the last he had earned… All the money was handed over to the checkpoint, to whoever was in command there." For a second "corridor", for Chechen civilians to leave the village, the Russian servicemen demanded 10 thousand dollars. Chechens from Samashki told human rights defenders of Memorial that on 14 March the village head took weapons and 50 million rubles (10 thousand dollars) to Russian checkpoint No. 13. On the morning of 15 March, the occupiers gave the Chechens two hours to leave the village (see: "Sham Corridor") and began the assault[7].

In the spring of 1996, the occupiers and the puppet government in Grozny forced villages to sign "Protocols on Peace and Accord" and to surrender weapons (see: "Collective Punishment")[12]. Chechens in Gekhi, a village southwest of Grozny, paid Russian servicemen about a hundred million rubles when signing the "Protocol on Peace and Accord". In Mesker-Yurt, a village southeast of Grozny, the occupiers received a hundred thousand rubles from each house[7]. On 7 April 1996, Russian servicemen ordered Saidash Shamakhurov from Gikalovo, a village south of Grozny, to drive in his own car to Khankala, the main base of the occupation troops on the eastern outskirts of Grozny. On 27 April, the occupiers exchanged him for a captured Russian soldier (see: "Hostage-Taking"), and three days later returned his car to him, taking 2,000 dollars and one more prisoner for it[15]. In April 1996, the occupiers surrounded Shali, a town southeast of Grozny, and Russian soldiers at the post did not let Chechens out of the town, demanded money from them and fired into the ground in front of them. On 26 April, more than a thousand Chechen women and children left Shali on foot[12][10].

The 1995 report of the observer mission of Russian human rights organizations says that Russian guards extorted money where they held detained Chechens before sending them to filtration camps, because no one controlled these places and they had no official status[9].

Despite the "Memo", Russian servicemen and police officers kept extorting payments at checkpoints until the end of the war in 1996, and Chechens complained about them to human rights defenders[12].
