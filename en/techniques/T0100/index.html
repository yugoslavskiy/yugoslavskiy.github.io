Seizure of Religious Institutions

Aggressors may establish direct forcible and administrative control over local religious institutions, depriving them of canonical independence and subordinating them to their own spiritual centers. The elimination of independent jurisdiction makes it possible to remove authoritative national institutions (seizure of governance) and to use the religious apparatus for the long-term imposition of loyalty and the ideological consolidation of the occupation.

Procedures Carried Out


January 1685
C0008.T0100 →
C0008 Annexation of the Kyiv Metropolis by the Moscow Patriarchate (1685–1690)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0011 Moscow Patriarchate

The liquidation of independent jurisdiction to turn the church into an instrument of assimilation – the annexation of the Ukrainian church to the Muscovite one in 1686[1]. The Ecumenical Patriarchate later officially stated that these actions "were carried out entirely out of accord with the lawful canonical prescriptions"[2].

January 1839
C0059.T0100 →
C0059 Liquidation of the Greek Catholic Church (1839)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

The liquidation of the independent spiritual structure on the Right Bank in 1839: "in connection with the tsarist regime's liquidation of the Union in this region (1839), the monastery was closed, and its buildings destroyed"[3].

January 1927
C1178.T0100 →
C1178 Collectivization of Chechnya (1927-1936)
G0010 USSR
S0008 Government

The colonizer took from the Chechen clergy the donations of their congregations and closed their houses of worship. In the autumn of 1929 in Goity the colonizers were confiscating a mosque over an unfulfilled "grain assignment" - a quota of grain owed to the empire; Shita Istamulov, leader of the uprising in Shali, spoke of it: "Look what an outrage they are committing in Goity; they have announced: until you deliver the grain, we will take away the mosque for that time. What good is this, when the Chechen people is being robbed of its last faith..."[4]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that in 1929 in the Nadterechny district "an experiment begins in removing the mullahs from the distribution of the voluntary donations of Muslims for the benefit of the poor and the clergy - the zakat" - the empire handed the distribution over to its own peasant mutual-aid committees[5]. The colonizer destroyed the greater part of the mosques of Chechnya - the before-and-after count is given by historian K. G. Dendiev: before the Russian revolution Chechnya had "2,675 mosques, 60 thousand murids [disciples of sheikhs], members of various religious organizations, 38 sheikhs, 850 mullahs and 140 religious schools", - and the count of mid-1937: "401 mosques, not counting the kub ones [small neighborhood mosques]", at which "406 mullahs worked"[4]. Belov, commander of the occupation troops of the North Caucasus Military District, in the district command's report to the krai committee of the party (the "North Caucasus kraikom VKP(b)") on the results of the military operation of spring 1930, named the closing of mosques on the colonizers' orders among the causes for which the Chechen auls had risen in arms[6].

January 1933
C0079.T0100 →
C0079 Cultural Terror and the "Executed Renaissance" (1933–1938)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

Mass closure and destruction of churches, as well as repressions against clergy, aimed at the complete destruction of the Ukrainian Autocephalous Orthodox Church (UAOC)[7].

January 1965
C1182.T0100 →
C1182 "Only Pardoned": Alien Rule and the Suppression of Chechen and Ingush National Life (1965-1989)
G0010 USSR
S0008 Government

After the return of the Chechens from exile the coloniser did not allow a single mosque to be opened and, through its officials, decided who could pray and where. Muzarov, an official supervising religion, wrote in a report of 25 August 1983: "In Chechen-Ingushetia in the pre-October period [before 1917] Muslim ideology reigned supreme. There were 2,675 mosques, 60 thousand murids [members of religious brotherhoods], 38 sheikhs [religious mentors, heads of brotherhoods], 2,850 mullahs [Islamic clergymen] and 140 religious schools. After the restoration of the republic's autonomy in 1957 it so happened that on the territory of the Chechen-Ingush ASSR there was not a single mosque"[8]. The mosques did not vanish by themselves. Most of them the coloniser had closed before the war: historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that religious schools "after the closure of most mosques… moved into the homes of mullahs and Islamic scholars"[5]. Historian Dalkhan Khozhaev writes that during the exile "medieval crypts and pre-revolutionary mosques, tombs and shrines… were blown up"[9]; historians Akhmadov and Khasmagomadov add that in Grozny "the city mosque was destroyed"[5]. The surviving buildings the coloniser took over as clubs and warehouses. On 21 January 1967 277 believers of the Nazran district submitted an application to open a mosque in the village of Barsuki; Asaulka, an official supervising religion, reported to Moscow that the district administration, by its decision of 10 June 1967, had refused "to consider the application on the grounds that, as established by an inspection, 'many signatures under the application were made not by the persons indicated in the application but forged, and the surnames of some persons are fictitious'"[10]. On 11 November 1969 the government of the region, subordinate to Moscow, in a resolution "On Measures to Strengthen Control over Observance of the Legislation on Religious Cults", recorded that in the districts "ministers of the cult in a number of cases arbitrarily engage in building and opening prayer houses, hold collective rites in public places, organise pilgrimages of believers to so-called 'holy places'", while "effective measures aimed at the gradual cessation of their activity are not being taken"[11]. On 18 May 1977 Bobov, an official supervising religion, reported to Kerimov, the head of that government: "in the republic there are no Muslim religious associations or ministers of the cult registered with the authorities, while at the same time, contrary to the law, 263 Muslim religious societies and groups operate unofficially"[12]. On 19 August 1980 Bobov reported to Moscow: in the republic "there are 62 buildings of former mosques… 17 used as clubs and schools, 25 as storage premises, 8 as factory workshops, post offices, shops, 2 handed over to the use of believers"[13]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that the region's occupation authorities "directly forbade the opening of mosques in Chechen and Ingush villages, and did not allow the numerous murid [religious] communities to perform religious rites openly either"[5].
