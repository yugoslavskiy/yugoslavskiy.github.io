Censorship

Aggressors may impose strict control over all forms of public expression, book printing, and mass media. Banning the publication of literature without the sanction of appointed censors, filtering information, and repressing independent authors allow the colonizer to monopolize the information space. This prevents the spread of dissent and ensures that the aggressor's state ideology is imposed on the colonized society without alternative.

Procedures Carried Out


January 1685
C0008.T0101 →
C0008 Annexation of the Kyiv Metropolis by the Moscow Patriarchate (1685–1690)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0011 Moscow Patriarchate

The introduction of censorship control over Ukrainian book printing. Patriarch Joachim banned publications without his sanction and sent a reprimand for publishing books without prior permission[1][2], and in 1690 "the Council of the Russian Orthodox Church condemned the 'new Kyivan books' of Petro Mohyla, Kyrylo Stavrovetsky, Ioanykii Galiatovsky, Sylvestr Kosov, Lazar Baranovych, and others, laying on them a curse and anathema"[3].

January 1720
C0043.T0101 →
C0043 Ban on Ukrainian Book Printing (1720)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0008 Government

The introduction of censorship over book printing by Peter I's decree of 5 October 1720: "no new books whatsoever are to be printed except previous church editions, and even those old church books… are to be corrected before printing"[4].

January 1862
C0046.T0101 →
C0046 The Valuev Circular: Ban on the Language and Schools (1862–1869)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

The closure of the national press: the Ukrainian socio-political and literary journal "Osnova" (St. Petersburg, January 1861 - October 1862) came to an end: the plans to reorganize the publication were thwarted by the minister of internal affairs Valuev, who refused to sanction its publication under a renewed program[5].

January 1876
C0047.T0101 →
C0047 The Ems Ukaz and Total Censorship (1876–1887)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

The censor's refusal to consider national manuscripts: "On a Ukrainian grammar prepared in Russian, the censor wrote the resolution: 'It would be naive to hope for permission to print the grammar of a language that must not exist'"[6].

January 1892
C0049.T0101 →
C0049 Ban on the Language in Books and Public Speeches (1892–1905)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

Total censorship of translations and book imports: in 1892 the Main Administration for Press Affairs directed the censorship committee to suppress translations into Ukrainian, "reducing the number of such talentless works for purely state purposes"[7]; "The ban on importing Ukrainian books from abroad was confirmed in the harshest form in 1894"[6].

January 1913
C0051.T0101 →
C0051 Ban on Culture and the Press Before World War I (1913–1914)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

The purge of the information space: Nicholas II's personal decree of 20 July 1914 on military censorship gave the command the right "to forbid for a set term, by their own authority and within the locality subordinate to them, any assemblies whatsoever, and to suspend periodical publications, as well as the transmission of postal items and telegrams"[8]. On this basis the military administration of the Kyiv military district that very day closed the only Ukrainian daily newspaper, "Rada," and confiscated at the printing house the print runs of "Svitlo" and of the "Literary-Scientific Herald," and on 9 January 1915 all Ukrainian periodicals were shut down[9].

August 1914
C1189.T0101 →
C1189 First World War in Chechnya: removal of the "unreliable element" into the "Savage Division", extraordinary security and mobilization of strikers (1914-1917)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0012 Occupation and Controlled Administrations

On 16 August 1914, three days after placing the Terek oblast under "extraordinary security", the Russian Empire introduced in the oblast a provisional statute on military censorship[10][11].

January 1919
C0074.T0101 →
C0074 Soviet-Ukrainian War: Second Invasion and Resource Depletion (1919)
G0014 White Movement (AFSR)
S0012 Occupation and Controlled Administrations

On 18 November 1919 the Whites' city administration in Mykolaiv ordered preliminary censorship of printed publications[12]. So it was in Kyiv as well: an order of general Mai-Maevsky, head of the Whites' administration, subjected all publications to military censorship - publishing only with the governor's permission, with every issue presented to the prosecutor[13].

January 1933
C0079.T0101 →
C0079 Cultural Terror and the "Executed Renaissance" (1933–1938)
G0010 USSR
S0008 Government

Mass confiscation and destruction of literature declared "nationalist," and rigid control over the printed word and the arts[14].

August 1946
C0085.T0101 →
C0085 Postwar Ideological Terror ("Zhdanovshchina") (1946–1953)
G0010 USSR
S0021 Propaganda

Total ideological control over art and the press, the banning of works and the vilification of writers for alleged "bourgeois nationalism." The campaign culminated in the verdict in the case of the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee: "on July 18, 1952 the Military Collegium of the USSR Supreme Court passed death sentences on 13 people, including 5 natives of Ukraine, well-known Jewish writers and poets"[15].

August 1991
C0095.T0101 →
C0095 Attempted Coup d'État by the GKChP (August 1991)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

The immediate elimination of the independent press: GKChP resolution No. 2 of 19 August 1991 ordered "to temporarily limit the list of published central, Moscow city, and oblast socio-political editions to the following newspapers: 'Trud,' 'Rabochaya tribuna,' 'Izvestia,' 'Pravda,' 'Krasnaya zvezda,' 'Sovetskaya Rossiya,' 'Moskovskaya pravda,' 'Leninskoye znamya,' 'Selskaya zhizn'"[16] – anything not on the list could not appear.

September 1994
C1193.T0101 →
C1193 First Russian-Chechen War (1994–1996)
G0011 Russian Federation
S0008 Government

In the autumn of 1994, Russia attacked the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria under the name of a puppet opposition, and on 11 December 1994 the Russian army openly invaded the republic. Russia decided which journalists could work in Ichkeria, confiscated their footage and persecuted them for reporting on the war.

On 1 December 1994, ten days before the open invasion, Chernomyrdin, chairman of the Russian government, by directive No. 1886-r created the Temporary Information Center[17][18]. On 9 December, by resolution No. 1360, the Russian government instructed the center to issue journalists accreditation, the permit to work in Ichkeria, and to withdraw it immediately for information the center deemed false. The government gave the Russian Interior Ministry (MVD) and the FSK (predecessor of today's FSB) the right to expel from Ichkeria any outsiders they deemed dangerous[19].

On 29 December 1994, the Russian MVD announced that the Temporary Information Center's accreditation gave no right to travel where the Russian Internal Troops (predecessors of today's Rosgvardia) were stationed[18].

At the end of January 1995, the Russian government created the Mobile Information Center in Mozdok, a town in Russian-controlled North Ossetia where the occupiers' headquarters stood. Only this center's accreditation gave journalists the right to be in the "combat zone", and there only under the supervision of servicemen or officials. The occupiers usually counted all of Ichkeria as this zone[18].

The Russian military expelled journalists without accreditation from the republic, and detained journalists with accreditation and confiscated their tapes and equipment, explaining that accreditation from the Ministry of Defense or the MVD was also needed[18].

On 15 December 1994, Soskovets, deputy chairman of the Russian government, announced that the government could strip the NTV television channel of its broadcasting license for its reports on the victims of the war in Ichkeria. After public protest, the government dropped this threat[20].

At the end of May 1995, officers of the Russian FSB arrested NTV journalists near the stanitsa of Assinovskaya in the west of Ichkeria for filming Russian troop positions, although the commander of the Russian division had allowed it. The FSB held the journalists for six hours in a filtration camp and released them (see: "Filtration Camp System")[21]. On 3 June 1995, the Russian military arrested three foreign journalists on the border between Ichkeria and Russian-controlled Dagestan and reprimanded them for not having the proper accreditation. Four days later, on 7 June, the occupiers let the journalists go to Moscow but confiscated their tapes and film[20].

On 25 June 1995, Yelena Masyuk, a journalist of the Russian television channel NTV, recorded an interview with Shamil Basayev, a commander of the Armed Forces of Ichkeria, in the Nozhai-Yurt district in the southeast of the republic[20]. In April 1996, the journalist told the public tribunal of the Russian Glasnost Foundation in Moscow: "To get accreditation to work in Chechnya, you had to get to the military airfield in Mozdok, where the accreditations were issued — but to drive onto the airfield, you already had to have that accreditation." About the road to Shamil Basayev she told the tribunal: "To get to the Chechen village where, by my guess, Basayev was at the time, I had to walk 12 kilometers on foot around the Russian checkpoints"[21]. On 13 July 1995, the Prosecutor General's Office of Russia opened a criminal case against Yelena Masyuk for not telling the investigators where she had taken the interview. At the end of August, the prosecutor's office closed the case without charges and asked the Russian government's press agency to make sure such interviews did not happen again[20].

From 6 to 9 March 1996, while the Armed Forces of Ichkeria were attacking the occupiers in Grozny, Russian officials forbade television crews in the city to film[22]. On 8 August 1996, a Russian soldier at a checkpoint near Grozny told four journalists of foreign media to turn back and threatened to destroy their equipment, and when they turned around, the occupiers fired a grenade launcher at their car and missed. Two hours later, Russian pilots fired on the journalists from helicopters at another entrance to the city but wounded no one[22].

In its report for 1996, the international organization Committee to Protect Journalists wrote that throughout the war Russian troops refused to recognize journalists' accreditation, spoiled their film, destroyed their equipment and fired on their cars from checkpoints and combat helicopters[22].

In August 1996, the Armed Forces of Ichkeria liberated Grozny from the occupiers, forcing Russia to the negotiating table[23]. On 31 August 1996, peace agreements were signed in Khasavyurt (see: "Sham Treaties")[24]. On 31 December 1996, the last Russian troops left the republic[18].
