Censorship

Aggressors may impose strict control over all forms of public expression, book printing, and mass media. Banning the publication of literature without the sanction of appointed censors, filtering information, and repressing independent authors allow the colonizer to monopolize the information space. This prevents the spread of dissent and ensures that the aggressor's state ideology is imposed on the colonized society without alternative.

Procedures Carried Out


January 1685
C0008 Annexation of the Kyiv Metropolis by the Moscow Patriarchate (1685–1690)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0011 Moscow Patriarchate

The introduction of censorship control over Ukrainian book printing. Patriarch Joachim banned publications without his sanction and sent a reprimand for publishing books without prior permission[1][2], and in 1690 "the Council of the Russian Orthodox Church condemned the 'new Kyivan books' of Petro Mohyla, Kyrylo Stavrovetsky, Ioanykii Galiatovsky, Sylvestr Kosov, Lazar Baranovych, and others, laying on them a curse and anathema"[3].

January 1720
C0043 Ban on Ukrainian Book Printing (1720)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0008 Government

The introduction of censorship over book printing by Peter I's decree of 5 October 1720: "no new books whatsoever are to be printed except previous church editions, and even those old church books… are to be corrected before printing"[4].

January 1862
C0046 The Valuev Circular: Ban on the Language and Schools (1862–1869)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

The closure of the national press: the Ukrainian socio-political and literary journal "Osnova" (St. Petersburg, January 1861 - October 1862) came to an end: the plans to reorganize the publication were thwarted by the minister of internal affairs Valuev, who refused to sanction its publication under a renewed program[5].

January 1876
C0047 The Ems Ukaz and Total Censorship (1876–1887)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

The censor's refusal to consider national manuscripts: "On a Ukrainian grammar prepared in Russian, the censor wrote the resolution: 'It would be naive to hope for permission to print the grammar of a language that must not exist'"[6].

January 1892
C0049 Ban on the Language in Books and Public Speeches (1892–1905)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

Total censorship of translations and book imports: in 1892 the Main Administration for Press Affairs directed the censorship committee to suppress translations into Ukrainian, "reducing the number of such talentless works for purely state purposes"[7]; "The ban on importing Ukrainian books from abroad was confirmed in the harshest form in 1894"[6].

January 1913
C0051 Ban on Culture and the Press Before World War I (1913–1914)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

The purge of the information space: Nicholas II's personal decree of 20 July 1914 on military censorship gave the command the right "to forbid for a set term, by their own authority and within the locality subordinate to them, any assemblies whatsoever, and to suspend periodical publications, as well as the transmission of postal items and telegrams"[8]. On this basis the military administration of the Kyiv military district that very day closed the only Ukrainian daily newspaper, "Rada," and confiscated at the printing house the print runs of "Svitlo" and of the "Literary-Scientific Herald," and on 9 January 1915 all Ukrainian periodicals were shut down[9].

August 1914
C1189 First World War in Chechnya: removal of the "unreliable element" into the "Savage Division", extraordinary security and mobilization of strikers (1914-1917)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0012 Occupation and Controlled Administrations

On 16 August 1914, three days after placing the Terek oblast under "extraordinary security", the Russian Empire introduced in the oblast a provisional statute on military censorship[10][11].

January 1933
C0079 Cultural Terror and the "Executed Renaissance" (1933–1938)
G0010 USSR
S0008 Government

Mass confiscation and destruction of literature declared "nationalist," and rigid control over the printed word and the arts[12].

August 1946
C0085 Postwar Ideological Terror ("Zhdanovshchina") (1946–1953)
G0010 USSR
S0021 Propaganda

Total ideological control over art and the press, the banning of works and the vilification of writers for alleged "bourgeois nationalism." The campaign culminated in the verdict in the case of the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee: "on July 18, 1952 the Military Collegium of the USSR Supreme Court passed death sentences on 13 people, including 5 natives of Ukraine, well-known Jewish writers and poets"[13].

August 1991
C0095 Attempted Coup d'État by the GKChP (August 1991)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

The immediate elimination of the independent press: GKChP resolution No. 2 of 19 August 1991 ordered "to temporarily limit the list of published central, Moscow city, and oblast socio-political editions to the following newspapers: 'Trud,' 'Rabochaya tribuna,' 'Izvestia,' 'Pravda,' 'Krasnaya zvezda,' 'Sovetskaya Rossiya,' 'Moskovskaya pravda,' 'Leninskoye znamya,' 'Selskaya zhizn'"[14] – anything not on the list could not appear.
