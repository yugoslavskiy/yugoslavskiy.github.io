Pillage

Aggressors may loot: appropriate residents' property during assaults, sweep operations and searches, divide the "spoils" between soldiers and commanders, carry off livestock, household goods and valuables. Pillage is not limited to combat: on occupied territory it is any appropriation of residents' property - by soldiers or by representatives of the aggressor's administration - for private or institutional benefit without the owner's consent. The Hague Regulations forbid giving up a locality to pillage "even when taken by assault" (Article 28) and "formally forbid" pillage on occupied territory (Article 47); pillaging a town or place is a war crime (Rome Statute, Article 8(2)(b)(xvi)), and both organized seizure of property and acts of individuals for private gain qualify as pillage (ICTY, Celebici case, 1998).

Representatives of the metropole's authorities openly seized property from the Nokhchi population, abusing their official position: in their petition to Tsar Mikhail Fyodorovich, the service Okochans of the Terek town reported that the Kabardian prince Sunchaley Yanglychevich Cherkassky «having taken from us by force 8 good horses from the herd, sent them off to his kabaks (villages) in Kabarda»[1][2].

C1120 Punitive campaign and the devastation of the Nokhchi mountain communities (1617–1618)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0012 Occupation and Controlled Administrations

The punitive detachments plundered and enslaved the population: in a petition to Tsar Mikhail Fyodorovich, the Kabardian prince Sunchaley Yanglychevich Cherkassky reported that they «utterly ruined» the auls «and took many captives»[2].

September 1818
C1136.T0164 →
C1136 Exploitation of the village of Solzha (Sunzhenskaya) as a resource base and its punitive devastation for the armed resistance of the Nokhchi (1818)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

Solzha (Sunzhenskaya), one of the wealthiest villages, the Russian Empire kept as a source of supply for the fortress of Groznaya, and having seized the village by force, it spent several days carting away its stores. Historian D. A. Khozhayev records the ravaging of Solzha (Sunzhenskaya), as a consequence of which "most of the peaceful neighboring auls fled into the mountains, and the flourishing banks were left deserted for a long time thereafter"[3]. Imperial general Yermolov testifies in his "Notes" that he had earlier ordered the troops to spare the village so that the fortress "could obtain from it all necessary supplies," and when the troops seized the village, "grain, forage, and timber fit for building were carted away for several days"[4].

July 1819
C1143.T0164 →
C1143 Demonstrative destruction of the aul of Dadi-Yurt and the construction of the Vnezapnaya fortress to force the Nokhchi off the Kumyk plain (1819)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

When laying waste to a Nokhchi aul, the Russian Empire handed its property over to the soldiers as spoils. Historian D. A. Khozhaev writes that the punitive raids under Yermolov proceeded "with the destruction of the population, houses, crops, orchards, and forests, the driving off of livestock, and the plundering of property," while Russian commanders invaded peaceful lands "to distinguish themselves or to seize booty"[3]. General Yermolov himself admits this in his "Notes": after the destruction of Dadi-Yurt, "the soldiers came away with rather rich spoils"[4].

G0009 Russian Empire

The Russian Empire drove off livestock and plundered the ravaged villages of the Nokhchi. Historian D. A. Khozhaev writes that by May 18, 1826, «livestock had been driven off», and after the troops returned, Yermolov «sent a detachment of 500 Cossacks against Daut-Martan», and «the village was ravaged and plundered»[3].

September 1830
C1152.T0164 →
C1152 Burning of Lowland Chechnya by Velyaminov's Expedition: Demolition of Villages from the Sunzha to Mairtup, Seizure of Astemir's Family, Collective Penalties Imposed on Chechen Villages, the Cutting Down of Fleeing Inhabitants of Dzulgai-Yurt, and Destruction of Winter Stores (1830-1831)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

In December 1830, during the encirclement of the village of Daut-Martan, the troops of General Velyaminov appropriated the property of the fleeing inhabitants: the imperial historian Potto records that "the inhabitants managed to flee, but all their property became the booty of the detachment"[5].

December 1831
C1154.T0164 →
C1154 Velyaminov's winter raids on Chechen hamlets and villages along the Sunzha: capture of women and devastation of homesteads (1831-1832)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

On December 23, 1831, the detachment of Lieutenant Colonel Zass, commander of the Mozdok Cossack Regiment, dispatched by General Velyaminov, drove off the livestock of the inhabitants of the devastated hamlets near the village of Chertugai. The imperial historian Volkonsky wrote: Zass "delivered to the camp... up to 250 head of cattle"[6]. Driving off 250 head of livestock in the middle of winter deprived the inhabitants of the devastated hamlets of their basic means of subsistence.

August 1832
C1155.T0164 →
C1155 Extermination of 61 settlements of lowland Chechnya and mountainous Ichkeria by Rosen's troops, burning alive of the defenders of Germenchuk, and extortion of hostages from 80 villages (1832)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

In the campaign of August - September 1832 the troops of corps commander Rosen appropriated the property and livestock of the residents of the villages being devastated. The imperial historian Volkonsky wrote: the property of 50 families who had taken refuge in the forest near Mairtup "fell as booty to the detachment", and near Tsentoroy "6 horses and 50 head of cattle were seized"[6]. Rosen reported to Minister of War Chernyshev that on September 15, near the village of Dungen-yurt, the cavalry "seized 140 sheep and 9 head of cattle"[7].

In the course of the extermination of Kishkeroy-Yurt on 28-31 December 1836, Pullo's detachment appropriated the inhabitants' property, including weapons taken from the bodies of the slain: "all the property of the inhabitants and the weapons taken from the slain fell to our troops"[8].

February 1837
C1161.T0164 →
C1161 Coercion of Chechnya into Oaths by Feze's Spring Tour: Amanats from Communities Coerced into the Oath, Destruction of Auls along the Dzhalka, a Livestock Fine (February-April 1837)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

Throughout Feze's spring tour (February-April 1837), the troops took the Chechens' livestock and property. According to Rozen's reports to Minister of War Chernyshev: on 5 February the livestock of mullah Udi's detachment was taken - "148 head of livestock of various kinds"; on 11 February "213 head of livestock of various kinds belonging to the unsubmissive Chechens were seized"; against those hiding in the forests "searches were made in various directions... and livestock of various kinds and property were seized from many of them"[7]. The April raid on the Gertli settlement, according to the report of 30 May, took "465 head of small livestock"[7].

November 1838
C1164.T0164 →
C1164 Forcing Chechnya into submission after the fall of Akhulgo and suppression of the general uprising of 1840 (1838-1840)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

In the marches of 1839-1840 the troops fed themselves on cattle taken from the Chechens and carried off the goods of the auls they were devastating. Of the December 1839 march of the detachment of general Pullo, who commanded the left flank of the Caucasus Line (the chain of imperial fortifications on the land of the Nokhchi), the imperial historian Yurov wrote: "on the march the men subsisted on ration cattle taken from the natives by way of fine"[9] - the army supplied itself with the population's property, framing the seizure as a fine. In the summer of 1840 the troops of general Galafeyev plundered the auls of Lesser Chechnya they were devastating: historian D. A. Khozhayev - "The detachment rode out onto the clearing with the goods looted in the devastated auls"[3]. In November, at the destruction of the aul of Mirti-Irzau, the imperial historian Yurov recorded: "150 head of horned cattle were seized"[9].

June 1840
C1165.T0164 →
C1165 "Constriction of Chechnya from all sides": blockade, secret war, punitive expeditions and the Sunzha Line (1840-1846)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0010 Regular Army

From the cut clearings and the new fortifications imperial detachments swooped on the Chechen auls, plundering and burning them. Historian N. I. The plunder here is not an excess but the regular mode of operations of the troops from the line.

April 1920
C1176.T0164 →
C1176 Sovietization of Chechnya: revkoms in place of Soviets, punitive operations and Cheka executions (1920-1924)
G0013 Soviet Russia (RSFSR)
S0010 Regular Army

In 1920 the Red Army looted the Chechen villages where it was stationed: the units quartered in Chechnya, historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write, "received almost no property or food allowance", which pushed them into "self-supply" - "...the Red Army not only ran riot, but took away grain, cattle and so forth, terrorizing the population with senseless killings"[12]. In the summer of 1920, in Tsentoroy, the Chechens routed a unit of the Caucasus Labor Army "engaged in outright plunder"[12]. The property of the killed was taken too: during the disarmament of May 1922 "up to 30 'bandits' were captured and shot, and their property was confiscated by the authorities"[12].

February 1943
C1180.T0164 →
C1180 Operation "Chechevitsa": the Total Deportation of the Chechen People and the Special Settlement Regime (1943-1949)
G0010 USSR
S0010 Regular Army

Deporting the Chechen people in February 1944, the colonizer looted its emptied homes. The Chechen historian Dalkhan Khozhaev writes: "In the days of the deportation, parallel to the human stream, columns of military trucks moved toward Grozny in an unbroken flow, loaded with the valuables looted from the emptied homes of the Chechens and Ingush. The items the guards deemed most valuable - carpets, burkas, Caucasian mounted belts, weapons ornamented with gold and silver (daggers, shashkas, flintlock rifles made by Chechen masters of the last century), jewels - were carried in canvas-covered vans"[14]. Drozdov, head of a brigade of the People's Commissariat (Ministry) of State Control of the RSFSR, who inspected the districts handed to Dagestan in August 1944 on the instructions of the government of the RSFSR, reported "unrestrained theft and squandering of the property of the special settlers," begun "soon after the deportation": Yusupov, acting chairman of a district soviet, "fully outfitted himself at the expense of the property of the special settlers" - he was short four hand-operated and four treadle sewing machines, 26 rugs, 30 carpets and 6 bicycles; Khashaev, prosecutor of Dagestan, received a house, an orchard, a vegetable garden and an "ownerless" cow; people's judge Suleimanov formalized his haul with a receipt: "Goods received, money not paid, to which I hereby subscribe"[14]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov sum up: "Implicated in the unrestrained theft of other people's goods was the entire party-economic and militia top brass of Grozny Oblast, Dagestan, Stavropol, North Ossetia and Georgia"; those caught were dismissed and tried, but "in the end the arrests had to be abandoned, since no one would have been left at liberty to govern"[12].

September 1994
C1193.T0164 →
C1193 First Russian-Chechen War (1994–1996)
G0011 Russian Federation
S0010 Regular Army

In the autumn of 1994, Russia attacked the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria under the name of a puppet opposition, and on 11 December 1994 the Russian army openly invaded the republic. The occupiers looted Chechen homes and carried the belongings away in military vehicles[15].

Officers of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs and soldiers of the Russian Internal Troops (predecessors of today's Rosgvardia), during "zachistkas" ("sweeps", as the Russians called house searches and detentions of Chechens) in Grozny, went from house to house with weapons. The occupiers took money, gold and valuables from the owners. Chechen families filed the most complaints of looting against them[15].

From December 1994, Russian police officers and fighters of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs looted Chechen families in the stanitsa of Assinovskaya in the west of Ichkeria[16]. In mid-January 1995, the elders let Russian troops into the stanitsa after they promised to do no harm (see: "False Guarantees of Safety"), and the occupiers looted the homes of Chechens[17]. On 28 and 29 January, the Russian military surrounded a block in the settlement of Butenko on the outskirts of Grozny with armored personnel carriers, went from yard to yard with weapons, took a thousand dollars from Leila Bapayeva, and from her neighbor, a mother of eleven children, her "Mother Heroine" medal[18][15]. In late January, the occupiers loaded appliances from abandoned houses of Grozny into armored personnel carriers, and a Russian captain drove a covered truck full of furniture through the city[15][16]. In February, because of the looting in Assinovskaya, the head of the Achkhoy-Martan district in the west of Ichkeria and a representative of the Armed Forces of Ichkeria refused to let the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs into the villages of the district[16].

In the winter of 1995, after the seizure of Grozny, the Russian military carried the belongings of Chechens on army vehicles through Ingushetia to North Ossetia, Russian-controlled republics to the west of Ichkeria. Russian traffic police recorded many such cases at their posts in Ingushetia[15].

In February 1995, Russian Justice Minister Kovalyov and General Babichev, who commanded one of the occupiers' groupings, declared on air that soldiers who take food from abandoned houses, or whatever is lying around unguarded, commit no crime[15].

On 1 March 1995, Sobolev, deputy director of the FSK (predecessor of today's FSB), admitted on the Vremya program of the ORT television channel that the Russian military in Ichkeria had looted. Russian soldiers who on 30 March 1995 seized Gudermes, the second-largest town of Ichkeria, took whatever they wanted at the market and did not pay[15][19]. Until December 1995, Russian servicemen who served in Gudermes on contract in 45-day shifts got drunk before going home, entered the homes of Chechens and took chickens, geese, carpets and dishes[19].

On 7-8 April 1995, the Internal Troops and police special forces of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs stormed Samashki, a village in the west of Ichkeria, and killed 103 peaceful Chechens (see: "Mass Killings of Civilians"). Along with armored vehicles, the occupiers brought military trucks into the village. The soldiers loaded video equipment, tape recorders, televisions, carpets and furniture into them and into armored personnel carriers, and searched women's necks for gold[16][20]. On 13 April, Sadulla Yusupov from Samashki told Oleg Orlov, a staff member of Memorial, how he climbed out of the cellar where a Russian serviceman had wounded him with a grenade and saw three occupiers in the yard: "They dragged out this little safe, this one here. We kept money and papers in it. Two of them are opening it with something, trying to open it, and the third is guarding them and shooting chickens, into the house". The soldiers opened the safe and left. Human rights defenders of the Observer Mission found Sadulla Yusupov's house burned down, but there were no traces of fighting on its walls[16][15].

In April 1995, Albast Madayev from Samashki wrote to Govorukhin, chairman of the Russian parliament's commission investigating the causes of the war in Ichkeria: "They burned my house with a shot from a flamethrower, killed 3 cows and 12 sheep. Of the household belongings, they took away what they could and set fire to the rest"[20]. The commission received 221 statements from Chechens of this village. In 60 of them it is said that the Russian military took valuables, food and shot livestock from the houses, and often burned the rest (see: "Total Destruction of Infrastructure"). On 6 June, Govorukhin, chairman of the commission, told Krasnaya Zvezda, the newspaper of the Russian Ministry of Defense, that he did not believe in the looting in Samashki. Not one of the complainants received an answer[16].

In April 1995, an officer of the Russian FSB confirmed in Ichkeria to Viktor Kurochkin and Viktor Sheinis, deputies of the Russian parliament, that soldiers of the occupation troops were looting the seized villages on a mass scale. The officer had filed a report about this to his superiors and received no answer[15]. On the night of 7 July 1995, the Russian soldiers Korovin, Shulin and Petrov came from their unit to the settlement of Rodina to demand money and gold from Chechens, killed two of them and wounded three. A year later, a military court gave Korovin 14 years, and Shulin and Petrov six and three years[21][15]. From 30 September 1995 to March 1996, police officers and soldiers of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs who kept Sernovodsk, a village in the west of Ichkeria, encircled looted Chechen families and drove away their livestock (see: "Transport Blockade")[22]. On 14 December 1995, the Armed Forces of Ichkeria liberated Gudermes, and on 19-20 December Russian troops seized the town again. Russian soldiers lived in dachas outside the town, came every day into the homes of Chechens and took carpets, dishes, food and Red Cross medicines[19].

In February 1996, the Russian military looted the houses that had survived the stormings in the settlement of Novogroznensky in the east of Ichkeria[15][22]. On 9 and 10 March, the Russian military carried mattresses, refrigerators and other belongings of Chechen families out of Sernovodsk on some thirty trucks, which doctors of the international organization Doctors Without Borders counted. At the resort north of the village, Russian soldiers looted and set fire to the apartments where refugees were living[22]. In March 1996, Russian soldiers looted the surviving houses in Samashki[22][15]. Russian guards in the prison of Pyatigorsk, a town in the south of Russia, took fur hats, leather jackets, shoes, money and gold from 91 Chechens whom the occupiers had unlawfully detained near Samashki on 15 March 1996 (see: "Filtration Camp System"). On 19 April, the Ministry of Internal Affairs released them without charges[23][15]. In August 1996, when the Armed Forces of Ichkeria were liberating Grozny, the reconnaissance battalion of the Russian army's 205th Brigade drove townspeople out of the 16-story buildings on Altaiskaya Street near the old airport, where there was no fighting, and loaded their belongings into vehicles. When the fighting ended and the soldiers left, the townspeople found almost all the apartments looted[15]. On 17 August 1996, soldiers of the 101st Brigade of the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs searched the houses around the "15th Gorodok", their base in Grozny, took whatever they wanted and shot up the rest with assault rifles[24].

On 20-22 August 1996, Aleksei Karmayev, a 67-year-old disabled man from Grozny, went to the headquarters of the 276th Regiment of the Russian army to demand that Russian soldiers stop looting and return his belongings. On 24 August, eight soldiers of the regiment led him away from the yard of his house on Kommunisticheskaya Street, after which the disabled man went missing (see: "Abduction of People")[23][25].

Human rights defenders of Memorial write that soon after the start of the war the occupiers began to loot and destroy the property of Chechens on a mass scale, and that Russian commanders and military prosecutors did not seriously combat the looting. By Memorial's conclusion, soldiers went on looting even after the leadership of the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs, in an address to the troops in February 1995, acknowledged looting, extortion and violence against peaceful Chechens[15].

In August 1996, the Armed Forces of Ichkeria liberated Grozny from the occupiers, forcing Russia to the negotiating table[26]. On 31 August 1996, peace agreements were signed in Khasavyurt (see: "Sham Treaties")[27]. On 31 December 1996, the last Russian troops left the republic[15].
