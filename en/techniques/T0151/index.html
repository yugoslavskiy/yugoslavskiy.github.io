Imposition of Alien Governance

T0151 Seizure of Governance, Persistence Nokhchi (Chechens)

The occupation administration artificially deprives subordinated societies of the right to self-governance, appointing loyal representatives of foreign elites over them. Unlike the creation of puppet governments (T0037), this technique does not aim to simulate a civil conflict or to create the appearance of a legitimate local authority. The use of alien overseers is employed by the metropole for open domination, the destruction of the Indigenous people's traditional social ties, and the uninterrupted mobilization of resources.

1661–1676 Establishment of Alien Governance and the Mobilization of the Nokhchi for the Crimean Campaigns Nokhchi (Chechens)

Establishment of Alien Governance and the Mobilization of the Nokhchi for the Crimean Campaigns (1661–1676) Tsardom of Muscovy Occupation and Controlled Administrations

The occupation administration of the Tsardom of Muscovy maintained the regime depriving the subordinated societies of the Nokhchi (the Okochans) of autonomy, officially formalizing the transfer of power over them to a new feudal lord loyal to the metropole: in 1661 Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich issued a charter of grant to the Kabardian prince Kaspulat Mutsalovich Cherkassky, instructing him «to be prince over the Okochans and over the Cherkas who serve us, the great sovereign, on the Terek, and to administer and judge them in military organization and in all our affairs»[1].

1858–1917 "Military-Popular Administration": the Colonial Regime of the Terek Oblast Nokhchi (Chechens)

"Military-Popular Administration": the Colonial Regime of the Terek Oblast (1858-1917) Russian Empire Occupation and Controlled Administrations

Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: "in 1867 Major General Prince Tumanov was appointed head of the Middle military otdel, while of the eight heads of the mountain okrugs six were colonels and two were majors"[2]. The head of the Terek oblast united civil, military and Cossack authority in one pair of hands and, in the words of the same historians, could against the highlanders "at his own will use... arms and raise against them the troops and Cossacks subordinate to him, hand highlanders over to a military court and administratively expel 'undesirable elements' from the oblast"[2]. From September 1866 the viceroy received the right to replace highlanders in the posts of pristavs (superintendents appointed by the empire) with incomer officers - historian Z. Kh. Ibragimova records the result: for a long time highlanders could rise no higher in the administrative system of the Terek oblast than the office of village elder[3]. The elders themselves were hand-picked by the authorities and sworn to "unquestioning execution... of all instructions and demands of the administration"; the population was left no choice - "The local authorities did not consider it possible to grant even the right to elect aul elders, since they 'found no sufficient guarantees that this would not entail serious inconveniences in the matter of governing the highlanders'"[3]. The court system was crowned by the same officers: in 1862 an oblast Main People's Court was created, all of whose members were appointed by the head of the oblast, and in 1870 - okrug "Mountain verbal courts" chaired by officers of the administration; historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov conclude: "Thus the court system created for the highlanders of the Terek oblast was not merely not removed from the control of the military authorities but, on the contrary, directly subordinated to them"[2]. The 1895 regulation on village communities handed the right of assembly to a limited number of "elected representatives", and the okrug head could strip an "elected representative" of his office and dissolve the entire village assembly[2].

1949–1965 "Exiled Forever": Life in Special Settlement and Return to an Occupied Homeland Nokhchi (Chechens)

"Exiled Forever": Life in Special Settlement and Return to an Occupied Homeland (1949-1965) USSR Government

The colonizer kept the highest posts of the restored republic for Russian leaders. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that around the first secretary of the Bolshevik party's oblast committee (the "obkom of the CPSU") Yakovlev "there formed a grouping of leading officials which had a negative attitude '...toward the act of restoring the autonomy of the Chechen-Ingush people...'," and Yakovlev himself "openly declared that he considered the decision on the rehabilitation of the repressed peoples a great mistake"[2]. An official of the government of the RSFSR, Fedin, reported on 29 August 1957 that three members of the Organizing Committee from among the Chechens and Ingush - Dukuzov, Malsagov and Tepsaev - "for more than eight months have not been appointed to any posts and in essence are responsible for nothing concrete," although "the republic feels a great shortage of cadres from among the Chechens and Ingush"[4]. The writer A. E. Kosterin, a witness of the events, wrote: "Everything was and is being done so as to provoke excesses on the part of the exiles both against the party-soviet organizations and against those who settled their villages... These excesses have been, are and, regrettably, will be, unless the position and tactics of the obkom change"[2]. The same historians write: "Very quickly an unspoken system of distributing the highest posts takes shape in socialist Chechen-Ingushetia" - the posts of first secretary of the party's oblast committee, minister of internal affairs, prosecutor, head of the KGB (predecessor of today's FSB), head of "Grozneft" and others "are reserved for Russian leaders"[2].

1965–1989 "Only Pardoned": Alien Rule and the Suppression of Chechen and Ingush National Life Nokhchi (Chechens)

"Only Pardoned": Alien Rule and the Suppression of Chechen and Ingush National Life (1965-1989) USSR Government

After the return of the Chechens and the Ingush from exile the USSR did not let the indigenous inhabitants hold key leading posts in government and industry, reserving them for Russian settlers. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: "The leading posts (first secretary of the obkom of the CPSU [head of the region's occupation administration], minister of the MVD, republican prosecutor, head of the KGB [predecessor of today's FSB], minister of finance, secretary of the Grozny gorkom of the CPSU [head of the city] and chairman of the Grozny gorispolkom [head of the city administration], head of Grozneft, military commissar of the republic and some others) are reserved for Russian officials"; for the Chechens and the Ingush, 70 % of the population, "roughly 30% of posts were reserved"[2]. According to the letter of 26 communists of 1972, which the historians quote, "in the early 1970s, of the 60 ministries and agencies of the Council of Ministers of the ChIASSR [the Chechen-Ingush republic] only 17 were headed by Chechens or Ingush. In most rural districts the first secretaries of the raikoms [heads of districts] were Russians"[2]. The historians write: "That is precisely why the CC of the CPSU [the central authority in Moscow] keeps sending new first secretaries of the obkom [heads of the region] to Chechen-Ingushetia" - in 1966-1989 these were Apryatkin, Vlasov and Foteev, sent in from other regions[2]. When the Ingush at the rally of January 1973 demanded that locals be appointed to leading posts, Bokov, a leader in the region's occupation administration, replied at a meeting of party functionaries on 21 January: "In their speeches they went so far as to say that a measure should be carried out here to take account of leading cadres from among the Chechens and the Ingush, that is, to carry out indigenisation"[5]. Historian A. B. Kuznetsova writes, citing the witness B. Kh. Toriev: "Chechens and Ingush were subjected to harsh discrimination in admission to the CPSU - they were admitted only from among the workers, so that those who held engineering positions were forced to go and work at a factory as labourers in order to be able to join the party"[6]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that only by the mid-1980s did Chechens and Ingush head 155 of the 194 village soviets and 11 of the 19 city and district soviets, while "the most significant posts at the middle and republican level, as well as the leadership of Grozny and of the regional committee of the CPSU, remained with the Russian nomenklatura"[2]. The historians write that on 1 July 1989 the region's occupation administration at its meeting "for the first time in the entire history of the Chechen-Ingush party organisation rejected the candidate proposed by the CC of the CPSU (N. I. Semenov) and elected Doku Zavgaev first secretary"[2].
