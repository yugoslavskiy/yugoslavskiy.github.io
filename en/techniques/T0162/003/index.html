Reprisals against Prisoners and Detainees: Killing of Prisoners and Detainees

Other sub-techniques of Reprisals against Prisoners and Detainees (4)
ID Name
T0162.001 Denial of Quarter
T0162.002 Killing of Those Who Surrender
T0162.003 Killing of Prisoners and Detainees
T0162.004 Torture and Abuse

Aggressors may kill prisoners and detainees - people already in their power: without trial, by lists, «while attempting to escape». The wilful killing of a prisoner of war is a «grave breach» of the Third Geneva Convention (Article 130), of a civilian in a party's hands - of the Fourth Convention (Article 147); the killing of any detainee outside combat is prohibited by Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions.

December 1917
C0019.T0162.003 →
C0019 The Battle of Kruty and the Killing of Prisoners (January 1918)
G0013 Soviet Russia (RSFSR)
S0010 Regular Army

The Red Guards shot the prisoners taken at Kruty (a station in the Chernihiv region) with the authorization of Yegorov, commander of the First Revolutionary Army. Lapidus, commissar of the 1st Moscow Red Guard detachment, who was with the prisoners at the station and the day before, together with Zombart, commander of the armored train, had talked the Red Guards out of an immediate reprisal, recounted in his testimony to the investigation in the case of commander-in-chief Muravyov: "The next day, as we were pulling out of Kruty station, the train stopped on the way at Yegorov's order; all the detainees were led out of the carriage, and 300 paces from the train they were shot with explosive bullets. I went up to the dead, then went up to Yegorov and asked why they had been shot. Yegorov, puffing on a cigarette and smiling, replied that he had given the Red Guards permission because they insisted on it"[1]. Moiseyev, a member of the committee of the First Revolutionary Army, described in his testimony who was shot: "At Kruty station about 20 haidamakas were taken prisoner, among them many gymnasium pupils of about 17. They were taken beyond the station and shot in the most shameless manner. There was an outrageous case when one youth ran away from that place, but he was chased down, brought back to the same spot wearing only his underwear and shot" - and specified: "this was done from the train commanded by Yegorov"[1]. Babenko, commander of the Moscow detachment, captured the prisoners, and Muravyov had appointed Yegorov commander of the First Army - both links are recorded in the investigation materials[1]. The investigation clarified the number of those shot: Tsvanger, secretary of the army committee, corrected her testimony, naming 24 people instead of the earlier 150, and a member of the investigative commission certified the correction[2]. The sources differ in describing the manner of the killing: Sotnyk Averkii Honcharenko, who commanded the Ukrainian detachment at Kruty, wrote in his memoirs that the prisoners were not shot but bayoneted, whereas the documents of the Soviet investigation record shooting with explosive bullets[2].

January 1919
C0074.T0162.003 →
C0074 Soviet-Ukrainian War: Second Invasion and Resource Depletion (1919)
G0013 Soviet Russia (RSFSR)
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

Retreating from Kharkiv in June 1919, the Bolsheviks' secret police killed its own prisoners and hostages - Ukrainians held in its jails without trial. By the count given in a summary of the Whites' Information Section, over the months of the Bolshevik occupation of Kharkiv more than 1,000 people were shot: 40-50 were killed daily, "and in the last days this figure rose sharply"; among the named dead were former governor Bantysh with his son, generals Nechaev and Kuskov, prince Putyatin[3]. Excavations after the Bolsheviks left yielded 239 bodies; forensic medical examination established facts of burial alive, of torment and of torture. In the concentration camp the secret police had set up on Chaikovska street, 33 bodies of executed hostages were dug up[3]. A witness from the camp hostile to the Whites - the left SR Karelin, held in the Kharkiv prison - confirmed both the routine and the last day: Saenko, commandant of the Kharkiv secret police, "usually shot all the condemned... with his own hand," and "on the last day of our stay in the Kharkiv prison... 120 people were shot in the back yard of our prison"[4]. There were no sentences: Kharkiv secret police investigators Portugeis and Feldman, by the documents studied by the historian Melgunov, "in 1919 shot people without any protocols at all" - by a pencilled resolution, "Baeva, as an incorrigible criminal, to be shot"[4]. Before leaving neighbouring Vovchansk the Bolsheviks shot 64 hostages, among them the headmistress of the girls' gymnasium[3]. In the settlement of Velykyi Burluk in the same Vovchansk district the Bolsheviks executed the entire family of prince Vadbolsky - nine people, among them two 80-year-old women and an English governess; the compilers of the summary recorded: "they were stripped naked, led behind the house church, candles were lit, they were ordered to kneel and pray, and then, to whistling and shouting, their ears were cut off, they were hacked with sabres and, as the victims lost consciousness, finished off with shots"[3].

December 1919
C0104.T0162.003 →
C0104 Soviet-Ukrainian War: Third Invasion and the Final Soviet Occupation (December 1919 - November 1921)
G0013 Soviet Russia (RSFSR)
S0010 Regular Army

In the autumn of 1921 the soldiers of the Army of the Ukrainian People's Republic (UNR), who had retreated across the Zbruch River in November 1920, formed detachments and returned to Ukraine to raise an uprising against Soviet rule. This raid is known as the Second Winter Campaign[5]. On November 17, 1921, occupation troops under the command of Kotovsky encircled the UNR detachment near the village of Mali Mynky in the north of Zhytomyr Oblast[5].

The captured Ukrainians were taken to the neighbouring village of Bazar and locked in the church. A visiting commission of the Cheka - the secret police of Soviet Russia, predecessor of today's FSB - arrived from Kyiv to judge them; the Communists called it the «Extraordinary Five». Its resolution, signed in Bazar on November 22, 1921, was entered into the collection of orders to the troops of the Kyiv Military District as No. 2578 of November 26, 1921[6].

The Russian commission described the battle as follows: «Over 400 men were cut down in the battle and 537 captured, including the wounded… In all, 443 men appeared before the extraordinary commission (the Five); the rest died of their wounds before the commission arrived or were in grave condition and left until recovery»[6].

The commission recorded the motive for the execution in the same document: «Guided by the dictates of revolutionary conscience… and in order to prevent similar crimes… and to show the entire capitalist world and its hirelings that the power of the Soviets mercilessly punishes anyone who attempts to disrupt its peaceful work, the Extraordinary Five resolves: 1. The persons listed in the annex hereto, numbering 359, as malicious, active bandits - TO BE SHOT»[6].

The fate of the remaining Ukrainian prisoners the commission set out in the same document: forty-one men of the senior command and civil administration it ordered sent for further interrogation by the investigative organs, and forty-two former Red Army soldiers, captured a year earlier, to the special department of the Kyiv Military District for screening[6].

The resolution was signed by commission chairman Garkavy, members Livshits, Ivanov, Kotovsky and Frinovsky, and secretary Litvinov. Kotovsky, who put his signature under the death sentence, commanded the very troops that had encircled the detachment at Mali Mynky. The order for the district was signed by district commander Yakir, member of the district Revolutionary Military Council Zatonsky, and district chief of staff Pauka[6].

The Soviet command offered the prisoners a transfer into the Red Army; not one agreed[7][8]. The colonizers carried out the execution in front of people herded to watch: the day before, they forced local peasants to dig a long ditch in a ravine near Bazar, and brought in the residents of the surrounding villages to the execution site[6]. Before the ditch one of the commissars asked once more who wished to join the Red Army. From the ranks came the answer: «We will not serve you!.. Shoot!.. The people will not forgive you for this!»[6]. The condemned embraced and sang «Shche ne vmerla Ukraina» - the song that seventy years later, after Ukraine declared independence in 1991, became the national anthem of Ukraine and remains so to this day[9]. To the sound of the anthem, Soviet troops shot all 359 Ukrainian prisoners near the village of Bazar[6][7]. The testimonies were gathered by a courier of the Ukrainian underground who visited Mali Mynky and Bazar immediately after the execution[6].

The exact day of the execution differs between sources, ranging from November 21 to 23[6][8][5].

The list of all 359 executed men, name by name, was published by Sotnyk O. Shpilinskyi in Kalisz in 1932; the list was later reprinted together with the text of the resolution[6].

Historian Larysa Yakubova writes: «This execution became a tragic symbol of the Ukrainian Revolution of 1917—1921. The armed struggle of the regular UNR Army came to an end»[7].

April 1920
C1176.T0162.003 →
C1176 Sovietization of Chechnya: revkoms in place of Soviets, punitive operations and Cheka executions (1920-1924)
G0013 Soviet Russia (RSFSR)
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

The special department of the Caucasus Labor Army carried out mass shootings uncontrolled even by its own command: army commissar Vrachev complained that of them "...almost nothing is known not only to the Revolutionary Tribunal or the Political Department of the army, but to me or the army commander either"[10]. During the disarmament of May 1922 people were first captured and then shot: "up to 30 'bandits' were captured and shot, and their property was confiscated by the authorities"[10].

July 1925
C1177.T0162.003 →
C1177 The disarmament of Chechnya: a military operation with artillery shelling and bombing of auls (1925)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

The USSR shot nearly all those arrested in the course of the operation of 1925 - people already in the hands of the troops and the OGPU (the political police, a predecessor of today's FSB). Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: "The total number of arrested exceeded 300. Of the total number of arrested, 105 were shot on the spot; against 183 an investigation was opened, which also ended in extrajudicial shooting; 21 were exiled"[10]. Even the OGPU's own count found few "active" ones among them: the survey submitted to Stalin names 50 "active bandits and prominent counter-revolutionary figures", while "In all, 400 bandits and elements abetting them have been arrested"[11] - those shot were mostly not leaders but rank-and-file detainees whose guilt was established by no court. So was killed the operation's chief prisoner as well: imam Nazhmuddin Gotsinsky - the spiritual head of the resistance in Dagestan and Chechnya, detained on 5 September 1925 and sentenced in Rostov-on-Don - was shot, in the words of a highlander emigre writing in the Paris journal Prométhée in 1930, "Il fut fusillé sans jugement dans les souterrains du Guépéou de Rostov" - without trial, in the cellars of the GPU in Rostov[12].

January 1927
C1178.T0162.003 →
C1178 Collectivization of Chechnya (1927-1936)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

The USSR shot arrested Chechens - including captured leaders of the uprisings. In March 1932 the Nozhay-Yurt district of Chechnya revolted against the forced merging of households into kolkhozes ("collectivization"); historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write: "The five captured leaders of the uprising were soon shot"[10]. Next the Chekists came for the clergy - Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov continue: "Arrests befell some well-known Chechen sheikhs, and Kana-Sheikh Khantiev was shot in April 1933"[10]. Kana-Sheikh Khantiev was a spiritual mentor of Muslims (a sheikh) from the village of Lakha-Nevre, today's Nadterechnoye; researcher A. Dukhaev, quoted by historian K. G. Dendiev, writes of him: "He was arrested for the last time on 17 March 1933, at the age of 85... and on 9 April of the same year sentenced to the supreme measure of punishment. The sentence was carried out on 10 May 1933. In 1990 Kana-Sheikh Khantiev was rehabilitated"[13]. The sources diverge on the date of the execution: April or 10 May 1933. The count of those shot is not limited to the top: the Chechen historian A. G. Avtorkhanov, who worked in Chechnya in those years and later wrote about it in emigration, writes that in the case of the "Chechen nationalist center", fabricated by the OGPU (the political police, a predecessor of today's FSB), rank-and-file people were shot as well - how many, it is impossible to establish[14].

July 1937
C1179.T0162.003 →
C1179 The Great Terror in Chechen-Ingushetia (1937-1940)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

On 11 July 1937 the occupation authorities approved the number of people "subject to shooting" in Chechnya: 862 "kulaks" (peasants whom the colonizer deemed "well-off") and 555 "criminals" - 1,417 people in all[13]; operational order No. 00447 of Yezhov, the People's Commissar (minister) of Internal Affairs of the USSR, of 30 July records for the ChIASSR: 500 people - to be shot (the order's "first category")[15]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write of the executed writers: "of the 12 members of the Chechen-Ingush Union of Writers 9 were arrested, 7 convicted, among them four - S. Baduev, A. Dudayev, Sh. Aiskhanov, S. Oziev - sentenced to be shot"[10]. The NKVD (a predecessor of today's FSB) arrested one of those four - the writer Abdi Dudayev - on 24 November 1937 "without the prosecutor's sanction", on the standard charge of "anti-Soviet agitation", and on 2 December, by decision of the "troika under the NKVD of the ChIASSR", the Chekists shot him - eight days from arrest to killing; the finding on his case of 13 May 1958 states: "The investigation of Dudayev's case was conducted in violation of the law; confrontations with witnesses were not held"[13]. Moscow personally confirmed the lists of detainees for shooting: Stalin, Molotov and Zhdanov signed the list of 7 December 1937, and on 28 December the Military Collegium of the Supreme Court of the USSR passed 32 death sentences on it[13]; on 12 September 1938 the Politburo confirmed a new list of those subject to its court: of the 61 under investigation, 50 went under the case of the "bourgeois-nationalist center", and 55 of them stood "in the shooting article"[13]. On 29 June 1938 the Military Collegium sentenced a large group of the republic's technical intelligentsia to various terms, and 30 people to be shot - the Chekists shot them the same day[13]. The indictment in the case of Porubay - deputy People's Commissar (minister) of Internal Affairs of the republic, put on trial after the change of course - states: "Falsified the investigative cases of Khamzatkhanov, Aliev, Khasiev, Kadilov, Ukhuev, Mutushev; all these persons were sentenced by decision of the troika to VMN" - "VMN" meant execution by shooting[13]. The Chekists dumped the bodies of the killed into two trench-pits at the central cemetery of Grozny - the historian T. U. Elbuzdukayeva writes: there, "in October, November and December 1937", they dumped "the bodies of the shot"[13]. A. Bozhedomov opened the burial in the summer of 1990 - by excavations with the participation of members of the Chechen-Ingush "Memorial" and staff of the museum of local history[13].

February 1943
C1180.T0162.003 →
C1180 Operation "Chechevitsa": the Total Deportation of the Chechen People and the Special Settlement Regime (1943-1949)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

In April-May 1943 officers of the Soviet NKVD (predecessor of today's FSB), led by Kolesnikov, Deputy People's Commissar (Minister) of Internal Affairs of the republic, in the course of "anti-banditry operations" detained Chechens "suspected of ties with bandits" and shot them "unlawfully, without trial or investigation" - 25 people in two months, "citizens guilty of nothing whatsoever": in the village of Bachi-Yurt, on the orders of cavalry platoon commander Volosov, the convoy shot 17 of 19 detainees, among them a 15-year-old girl; in the village of Belty a convoy headed by Soviet militia section commander Dubasov shot four people "without any pretext whatsoever" and delivered their corpses to the district branch of the NKVD; at the Kuloi hamlet Meishvili, a battalion commander of the NKVD troops (predecessors of today's Rosgvardia), shot the brothers Baisar and Baim Bataev, Dzhamalani Pitalov and Mavla Chebiev[16]. The executioners wrote the shootings off as "attempted escape," and Kolesnikov issued "findings that the use of weapons had been proper"; all this was established by the Chief Military Prosecutor's Office following a letter from the Chechen Dokhtukaev, written from exile - the results were reported in 1957 to Bulganin, head of the government of the USSR, but no one was ever brought to court: the ten-year statute of limitations had expired[16].

September 1994
C1193.T0162.003 →
C1193 First Russian-Chechen War (1994–1996)
G0011 Russian Federation
S0010 Regular Army

In the autumn of 1994, Russia attacked the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria under the name of a puppet opposition, and on 11 December 1994 the Russian army openly invaded the republic. The occupiers killed detained Chechens without trial.

In early 1995, during "zachistki" ("sweeps", as Russians called house searches and detentions of Chechens) in Grozny, Russian servicemen shot peaceful Chechens dead right after detaining them[17]. Russian escorts killed them on the way, in the backs of trucks and in columns on foot[17][18].

At the bases and positions of their units, Russian servicemen beat detained Chechens to death or shot them. The occupiers secretly buried the bodies of the killed near their positions and did not return them to the families (see: "Desecration of the Dead")[17][19][20].

In early January 1995, Russian soldiers detained more than 60 men near the cannery in Grozny. On 3 January, an escort drove them to the filtration camp in Mozdok, a town in Russian-controlled North Ossetia. When one of the two trucks broke down, the guards laid everyone in the back of the second one, in six layers and more. Those lying in the lower layers suffocated, and the escort shot at those who tried to move[17]. Zakrail Vairayev rode in that truck. On 28 February 1995, in Nazran, a town in Russian-controlled Ingushetia, he told a human rights defender of the American organization Freedom House about the journey. The record of his testimony says: "The vehicle was carrying 63 people. On the way, two suffocated, and six were shot right there, in the vehicle. So the dead rode along with the rest all the way to Mozdok. They beat us the whole way"[21]. Among those shot dead in the back of the truck was Ruslan Kasayev, a young man from Grozny[17].

On 28 January 1995, Russian soldiers led the Khamidov brothers under escort across the grounds of a unit of the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs (predecessors of today's Rosgvardia) on Mayakovsky Street in Grozny. A neighbor heard one of the brothers shout that they were being led to be shot, and told their father Khusein Khamidov, who from January 1995 had been searching for people missing in Ichkeria and opening mass graves. From the neighbor's words, the father found the grave at the place the Russian military unit had left[17]. On 24 April 1996, in Moscow, Khusein Khamidov told the public tribunal of the Russian Glasnost Foundation: "On 2 April [1995] I dug up a burial of five bodies in the yard of a private house at 16 Mayakovsky Street, and among them were my sons. … Their legs were tied with rope, all their teeth were knocked out, there were marks of severe beatings on their bodies". Of other graves the brothers' father told the tribunal: "On the basis of the exhumations we carried out, I can state that people were shot at the former locations of [Russian] military units. They were taken away in galoshes, in house clothes, with pillowcases pulled over their heads. Many of the exhumed bodies had their hands and feet tied with barbed wire, and there were numerous knife wounds"[20]. On 17 May 1995, the prosecutor of the occupation administration in the Zavodskoy district of Grozny opened a case into the murder of the brothers. Russian military prosecutors received the case and suspended it because they had not found the culprits[17].

In April 1995, Russian servicemen detained three Chechens near the filtration camp on the northeastern outskirts of Grozny; they had come for a released relative. The relative found their bodies in a sewer pipe a kilometer and a half from there: the eyes of the killed had been gouged out and their ears cut off, and each had 15 bullet wounds[19]. On 7-8 April 1995, soldiers of the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs and Russian police officers, during searches in Samashki, a village in the west of Ichkeria, shot detained Chechen men in courtyards, and the Observer Mission of Russian human rights organizations named 27 of them (see: "Mass Killings of Civilians")[18]. The Russian escort that led detained Chechens from Samashki to a military unit in the hills north of the village shot three of them dead on the way: one of them, wounded, a soldier killed on the order of the escort commander[18][17]. On 9 May 1995, soldiers of the 503rd regiment of the Russian army detained three Chechens from Shali, a town southeast of Grozny, at a checkpoint. After the regiment left on 3 June, relatives found the bodies of two of them secretly buried in a field near Shali, and on 6 June the body of the third at a cemetery; all the bodies bore marks of torture[19][17].

On 23 February 1996, when the Russian 58th Army was advancing on Bamut, a village southwest of Grozny, soldiers of that army put Sharip Batayev, a Chechen from Arshty, a village in Russian-controlled Ingushetia, onto an armored personnel carrier. On 20 March, investigators from Ingushetia found his body in a shallow grave near the position of the reconnaissance troops of the 693rd regiment of the 58th Army: the occupiers had broken his wrists and shot him in the back of the head[17][20]. From 3 to 9 March 1996, Russian troops stormed Sernovodsk, a village in the west of Ichkeria, and soldiers detained a young Chechen woman in the street and took her into a neighboring house, where her body was later found[22][23]. On 20 March, seven Russian servicemen landed from a helicopter at a dairy farm near Nagornoye, a village close to Grozny, lined up the peaceful Chechens who were hiding in a barn and shot the youngest men. Sources differ: Human Rights Watch writes of two brothers killed, while the Chechen Women's Committee named five people shot at the farm and Umar Tuzhuyev, whom the servicemen threw alive out of the helicopter[24][25][20].

On 28 March 1996, the occupiers gave up three Chechens and the bodies of two more detainees in exchange for five of their soldiers, and on one of the bodies Sultan Kacheyev, a commander of the Armed Forces of Ichkeria, saw a bayonet wound (see: "Hostage-Taking")[24][25]. On 30 April, soldiers of the Internal Troops of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs detained Chechens, residents of the city, at a checkpoint in Grozny and threw them into a pit in Khankala, the main base of the occupation troops on the eastern outskirts of Grozny. The guards beat two of them to death; the rest were taken out of Khankala on 3 May by the Russian criminal investigation police, and a prosecutor of the puppet government confirmed that both had died of beatings and had had no connection to the army of Ichkeria[17]. On 12 May, Russian troops seized Kharsenoy, a mountain village in the south of Ichkeria, and soldiers shot dead on the road six young Chechens who had left the village, and did not return their bodies to their relatives. In the village, the soldiers shot dead another young Chechen who asked them not to enter the mosque[20]. On 17 August 1996, masked Russian servicemen searched houses near the republican hospital in Grozny. When the occupiers left the block, relatives found in a neighboring yard the bodies of two Chechens, cousins, with their hands tied with wire and marks of torture[26].

On 26 March 1996, the UN Secretary-General transmitted to the UN Commission on Human Rights reports that Russian troops had executed captured Chechen men without trial: peaceful people and soldiers of the army of Ichkeria[27]. The human rights defenders of "Memorial" write that officers of the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs and Russian servicemen executed people in Ichkeria without trial. In their conclusion, escorts and guards felt unpunishable in the first hours and days after a detention because the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs counted a detainee's term of custody from the hour he was brought to a filtration camp[17][18]. It is unknown how many detained Chechens the occupiers killed from December 1994 to August 1996. In early 1997, the human rights defenders of "Memorial" wrote of detained and missing Chechens: "Many of them have died, and now it is necessary to find the places of their burial, exhume and identify the bodies"[28].

Russian military prosecutors put Sokolov, the former commander of the 503rd regiment, on trial for the murder of three Chechens from Shali, but a military court returned the case to the prosecutors, and by the spring of 1998 they had not completed it (see: "Impunity of Perpetrators")[17].

In August 1996, the Armed Forces of Ichkeria liberated Grozny from the occupiers, forcing Russia to the negotiating table[29]. On 31 August 1996, peace agreements were signed in Khasavyurt (see: "Sham Treaties")[30]. On 31 December 1996, the last Russian troops left the republic[17].
