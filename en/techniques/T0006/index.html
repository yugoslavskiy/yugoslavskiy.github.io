Dehumanization

Aggressors may use propaganda practices to strip members of the colonized people of their human features. The victim is deliberately marked with derogatory labels (e.g., "subhumans," "militants," "Nazis," "fascists," "terrorists"), which is intended to remove psychological and moral barriers among the occupation troops and to legitimize any atrocities in the eyes of the aggressor's society, leading to a complete loss of empathy.

January 1721
C1108.T0006 →
C1108 Imposition of an Image of Backwardness and the “Civilizing” Mission (1721–1800s)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

The government of the Russian Empire deliberately constructed an image of the Indigenous population as criminal savages: historian Michael Khodarkovsky records that in administration documents the highlanders were labeled as "inconstant and treacherous," while their actions were explained by references to their "predatory craft, to which they are predisposed by their very nature and upbringing"[1].

January 1800
C1106.T0006 →
C1106 Propagandistic Inversion of Roles and Dehumanization (1800–1864)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

The apparatus of the Russian Empire constructed an image of the Indigenous population as savages: historian Ya. Z. Akhmadov points out that pre-revolutionary historiography described the highlanders' struggle as «the actions of barbarians devoid of 'civilized' notions of independence and freedom»[2], while the imperial historian and participant in the events V. A. Potto cites the words of General A. P. Yermolov, who declared that «the cruelty of the local mores cannot be tamed by mercy»[3]. In his «Notes», General Yermolov wrote of the Nokhchi: «the Chechens, the most vicious of the brigands, attacking the line», their land a refuge where «the villains of all other peoples, abandoning their own land on account of some crimes, were received amicably», while Chechnya itself «may justly be called the nest of all brigands», «were less filled than others with brigands who had previously taken part in all the raids of the Chechens on the line. In them predators gathered and took cover…»[4].

January 1858
C1022.T0006 →
C1022 Muhajirism: the expulsion of Chechens to the Ottoman Empire, the ban on return, and population replacement (1858-1872)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

The Viceroy of the Caucasus and commander-in-chief of the Caucasus Army Baryatinsky - on the Chechens who wished to leave: "...it is perhaps no bad thing that all this rabble, which is only a burden to us, will leave. Do not stop them, let them go to Mecca or wherever they wish. And I will take care to present this matter to the Sovereign in the proper light..." - to the emperor the matter was presented as "a voluntary movement of the Chechens themselves"[5]. The head of the Kuban oblast, General Yevdokimov, who carried out the expulsion of the highlanders of the Western Caucasus, formulated the principle: "Philanthropy first of all to our own; as for the highlanders, I consider myself entitled to grant them only what remains for their share after the last of Russian interests has been satisfied"[5]. The optic in which such decisions were taken was also articulated by imperial scholarship: Berzhe, who headed the Caucasus Archaeographic Commission for 22 years and compiled the first ten volumes of the "Acts"[6], grounded distrust of the highlanders in their nature: "The eight-hundred-thousand-strong highland population of the Terek and Dagestan oblasts forms here an almost solid mass... Permeated by Muslim fanaticism, inflamed by the long war, it continues to hate us as still-recent sworn enemies, as infidels, and will preserve this feeling as long as we remain giaours in its eyes"[7]. This is not an order but the voice of the epoch - the language in which the expulsion looked natural.

July 1925
C1177.T0006 →
C1177 The disarmament of Chechnya: a military operation with artillery shelling and bombing of auls (1925)
G0010 USSR
S0010 Regular Army

On 31 July 1925 the Revolutionary Military Council of the USSR explained the operation being prepared by the properties of an entire people - the order says: "The Chechen oblast is a hotbed of criminal banditry spreading to the districts neighboring Chechnya... In their mass the Chechens are inclined to banditry as the main source of easy gain, which is furthered by the presence of a large quantity of weapons. Mountain Chechnya is a refuge for the most hardened enemies of Soviet power"[8]. The label from the order passed into the operation's working nomenclature: the instruction of 4 August 1925, approved by Uborevich, commander of the North Caucasus Military District, Volodin, member of the district's Revolutionary Military Council, and Yevdokimov, plenipotentiary representative of the OGPU (the political police, a predecessor of today's FSB) for the krai, puts people in one row with rifles and "removes" them with a single verb - point 8 of the instruction reads: "If the conditions of the surrender of weapons are fulfilled, no house-to-house search is conducted, and only the bandit and vicious element is removed"[9]. Thus criminality was declared a property of the people, and its men - an "element" subject to removal.

September 1994
C1193.T0006 →
C1193 First Russian-Chechen War (1994–1996)
G0011 Russian Federation
S0008 Government

In the autumn of 1994, Russia attacked the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria under the name of a puppet opposition, and on 11 December 1994 the Russian army openly invaded the republic. Throughout the war, Russia called the army and the authorities of Ichkeria "bandits", "bandit formations" and "terrorists", and Chechens "militants".

On 2 November 1993, Russian president Yeltsin, by decree No. 1833, approved a military doctrine under which the army could be used against "illegal armed formations"[10][11]. With these words Russian officials designated the Armed Forces of Ichkeria[12].

On 27 December 1994, Russian president Yeltsin, in a televised address to the citizens of Russia, called some of the defenders of Ichkeria professional "terrorists" and mercenaries from other states. The president set Russian soldiers and police officers the task of disarming the "bandit formations". Yeltsin warned Russians that their relatives could fall victim to the "rampant banditry" in Ichkeria[13]. Commanders of the occupation forces called the people in Grozny and in the villages that the Russian army was pounding "bandit formations" and "militants"[12].

A soldier of the Russian border troops who fought in Ichkeria told the newspaper Rossiyskiye Vesti about retraining in Stavropol, a city in the south of Russia. There the soldiers were told to set booby traps without thinking about who would be blown up by them: a child would grow up to be a "mujahid", and a woman would give birth to a new "militant"[12].

On 7 April 1995, the Internal Troops of Russia's Ministry of Internal Affairs (predecessors of today's Rosgvardia) began the assault on Samashki, a village in the west of Ichkeria[14]. On 8 April, a Russian soldier was driving women and children out of a cellar on Vygonnaya Street. Mariet T., a Chechen woman from that street, told observers of Memorial that the occupier shouted at them: "These aren't women, they're bitches, whores, they kill ours too, and we're supposed to spare theirs?!" Then the Russian military herded some of the women into a room and others into a cellar and shot at them (see: "Mass Killings of Civilians"). Ruslan N. from Samashki recalled how MVD soldiers detained him that day: "They took my passport and right there, in front of me, tore it up… 'You'll go down as a militant. You've got a militant's mug'". At a military unit north of the village, the servicemen beat him unconscious. Ruslan N. survived[15].

Russian officials and military men, from the prime minister to the soldier conducting a search, called the authorities of Ichkeria "bandits"[16][17], its army "bandit formations"[12] and "terrorists"[18][19], and Chechen civilians "bandits"[20] and "militants"[21]. On 4 January 1996, Tikhomirov, commander of the occupation forces, in an interview with the Russian agency Interfax, spoke of Aslan Maskhadov, chief of the Main Staff of the Armed Forces of Ichkeria, as a "bandit"[12].

On 11 and 12 July 1996, unknown persons blew up two trolleybuses in Moscow[12]. According to Amnesty International, at the site of the second explosion Moscow mayor Luzhkov told a Russian police general that all Chechens should be expelled from the city. The general replied that if he were allowed to, he would introduce terror on the streets. The Russian television channel NTV showed this conversation on the day of the explosion[22][12]. At a press conference at the mayor's office, Luzhkov told journalists that a "Chechen trace" could be seen in the explosions[23][24][12]. Alexander Litvinenko, a former FSB officer, and the historian Yuri Felshtinsky write that those who blew up the trolleybuses "were not caught and… it was not determined whether they were Chechens or not"[25]. Human rights defenders of Human Rights Watch wrote in their report for 1996 that Luzhkov's televised statements "set the tone" for raids on markets and dormitories. In the two days after the explosions, the Moscow police, which verified documents "overwhelmingly against dark-skinned people", detained 5,770 people for violating residence registration rules[26]. In a 1999 report, Amnesty International wrote that for several years Luzhkov's statements served the police as an implied instruction to persecute and intimidate Chechens solely because they were Chechens[27].

At a hearing in the US Congress, the Russian journalist Yevgenia Albats said that those whom Russia called "bandits" were ordinary citizens who had stood up for their land, their houses and their families[28]. Human rights defenders of Memorial write that until June 1995, and again from late January 1996, Russia waged the war without calling it a war or the enemy an enemy[12].

September 2004
C0096.T0006 →
C0096 Election Interference and Countering the "Orange Revolution" (2004)
G0011 Russian Federation
S0021 Propaganda

The conduct of an aggressive information campaign in favor of "their" candidate with the direct participation of the head of the Russian state: live on three Ukrainian television channels two weeks before the vote, praise was voiced for the candidate's government - "V. Yanukovych's government achieved more: they managed to fill this growth with good quality. That is, Ukraine's economy is developing in a diversified way"[29]. In parallel, opponents were demonized with accusations of nationalism and pro-fascist views.

January 2020
C0102.T0006 →
C0102 Ideological Preparation for the Full-Scale Invasion (2020–2021)
G0011 Russian Federation
S0009 Diplomacy

An article by D. Medvedev, deputy chairman of Russia's Security Council, in Kommersant (11 October 2021) marginalizing the Ukrainian leadership: "it is pointless for us to deal with vassals. One must do business with the suzerain"; "at the head of Ukraine stand weak people who strive only to line their own pockets," and negotiations with them are "absolutely pointless"[30]. In the same text the Ukrainian president was likened to a hypothetical Jew serving in the SS[30].
