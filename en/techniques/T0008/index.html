Deprivation of Agency

Aggressors may deliberately deny an Indigenous people the right to independent political, legal, and historical existence. At the ideological level, this manifests in the dissemination of narratives about the colonized society's lack of agency, which serves the legitimization of domination. At the diplomatic and administrative level, the technique is implemented through the unilateral abolition of sovereignty, the annulment or rewriting of previously concluded international treaties, and the shift of relations from equal footing to a format of direct diktat, which ensures the institutional seizure of governance and the legal consolidation of the occupation.

January 1614
C1114.T0008 →
C1114 Deprivation of the Nokhchi of political agency and the imposition of alien governance (1614–1616)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0012 Occupation and Controlled Administrations

The metropole deliberately ignored official complaints by the Indigenous population about the arbitrary conduct of the appointed rulers, depriving them of the right to protection: the compilers of academic document collections state that, as regards the petition submitted to Tsar Mikhail Fyodorovich, «the petition of the «Okochans» was not granted»[1].

October 1656
C0002.T0008 →
C0002 Truce of Vilna (1656)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0009 Diplomacy

The Tsardom of Muscovy physically and legally isolated the autonomy from participation in international politics, deciding the fate of the Cossack lands without regard for their national interests: "The plenipotentiary representatives of the Ukrainian Cossack state, headed by R. Haponenko, were not admitted to the negotiating table"[2]. The hetman's request to grant the envoys mandates as full participants was "let pass unheeded" by the tsar's government: the delegates "sat idle beside the commissioners, not admitted to the official sessions"[3].

January 1667
C0005.T0008 →
C0005 Truce of Andrusovo (1667)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0009 Diplomacy

In the legal partition of Ukrainian lands, the opinion of the people themselves or of their leaders was demonstratively ignored. The Hetmanate was treated exclusively as an object: "The interests of the hetmans and of Ukraine were, of course, not taken into account"[4].

January 1686
C0009.T0008 →
C0009 "Eternal Peace" with Poland (1686)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0009 Diplomacy

The complete exclusion of the autonomy from participation in international negotiations on the status of its own territories. In the legal partition of Ukrainian lands the opinion of the people themselves was ignored – contemporary Ukrainian historiography assesses the Eternal Peace "as a national tragedy of Ukraine"[4].

January 1687
C0007.T0008 →
C0007 Kolomak Articles (1687)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0009 Diplomacy

"In this provision of the articles, the tsarist government's aspiration to turn Ukraine into a region incorporated into the Russian state on ordinary terms was openly declared for the first time"[4].

January 1700
C0011.T0008 →
C0011 Integration Reforms of Peter I and the Great Northern War (1700–1708)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0008 Government

A course toward the complete absorption and destruction of the very name of the Cossack state. In the Bendery Constitution of 1710, Orlyk recorded Moscow's intention "to eradicate the Zaporozhian Host of the Lower Dnipro and to efface its name forever"[5]. Hrushevsky confirms the course was systemic: Mazepa's alliance with the Swedish king merely "gave the Muscovite government occasion… to take up more forcefully the abolition of Ukrainian autonomy. The Muscovite government had pursued this line from the very beginning"[6]. What was at stake was the enslavement of the entire people: the Muscovite state, per the preamble of Orlyk's constitution, managed to "violate and utterly destroy" the rights and liberties "and to cast a slave's yoke upon the free Cossack people, never conquered by it"[7].

June 1709
C0015.T0008 →
C0015 The Battle of Poltava and the Final Defeat of the Hetmanate (1709)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0008 Government

The liquidation of the autonomy's statehood and international agency as a result of the defeat of the Swedish-Ukrainian forces. The rout at Poltava set off an irreversible process of dismantlement: "In the hundred years after the famous Pereiaslav Council, Ukraine traveled the path from the 'Articles of Bohdan Khmelnytsky'… to the de facto liquidation of the autonomy and the hetmanate, to the country's transformation into an ordinary province of the Russian Empire"[5].

July 1709
C0016.T0008 →
C0016 The Reshetylivka Articles (1709)
G0008 Tsardom of Muscovy
S0008 Government

Refusal to sign a treaty: the replacement of an equal agreement with the tsar's unilateral "resolutions." "The joint document, drawn up as a decree, was signed by state chancellor Count G. Golovkin"[8][9] – the autonomy ceased to be a party to a treaty.

January 1728
C0028.T0008 →
C0028 The Decisive Points (1728)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

Rejection of the treaty format: instead of a bilateral agreement – the tsar's "decisive" resolutions upon the hetman's requests, issued in the form of a decree. The autonomy ceased to be a party to a treaty and received its rules of existence from above.

November 1764
C0030.T0008 →
C0030 Liquidation of the Institution of the Hetmancy and the Second Little Russian Collegium (1764)
G0009 Russian Empire
S0008 Government

The final elimination of the office of the autonomy's leader: after Rozumovsky's forced abdication, Catherine II's decree of November 10, 1764 handed power to the Little Russian Collegium, whose president was instructed to act "with a governor's authority, that is, as our special plenipotentiary"[10]. Catherine II's instruction to procurator-general Vyazemsky (1764) stated the goal openly: "and when there is no hetman in Little Russia, one must strive that the very age and name of hetmans vanish"[11].

January 1919
C0074.T0008 →
C0074 Soviet-Ukrainian War: Second Invasion and Resource Depletion (1919)
G0014 White Movement (AFSR)
S0009 Diplomacy

The refusal of the White commanders to recognize Ukrainian statehood. General Denikin, as commander-in-chief of the Armed Forces of South Russia, signed the programmatic address "To the Population of Little Russia" (August 1919), which declared the struggle for a Ukrainian state to be treason. Symon Petliura (head of the Directory of the Ukrainian People's Republic and chief otaman of its army) and his associates, the address ran, "having begun the dismemberment of Russia, continue even now their evil work of creating an independent 'Ukrainian State' and of fighting against the rebirth of a United Russia"[12]. Denikin wrote in the first person: "declaring Russian the state language throughout the whole expanse of Russia, I consider entirely inadmissible and prohibit the persecution of the Little Russian folk language"[12]. The promised "prohibition of persecution" did not recognize the language but assigned it the place of a vernacular. What remained for Ukrainian under the address were everyday life, private schools, and the first years of primary school - Denikin explained: "to ease the pupils' assimilation of the first rudiments of knowledge"; the "treasonous movement" for statehood he required to be "completely distinguished" from the permitted "love for the native region… and its local folk language"[12]. Denikin allowed the people's existence and denied it political subjecthood: the striving for an independent Ukrainian state was declared treason, the language was left a vernacular. On the night of September 1, 1919, after four hours of negotiations, the Galician general Kravs and Denikin's general Bredov (according to the diary of the Supreme Command of the Ukrainian Galician Army - the commander of the Guards corps) agreed: the Galician units would withdraw from the city that same day "at least beyond the line of the Kyiv railway stations", then to the line Ihnativka - Vasylkiv - Hermanivka, while the Galician army guarantees that it "will make no moves against the army of the volunteers [Denikin's Volunteer Army]"[13]. In the fourth point the Galician command renounced political subjecthood: "The commander of the Kyiv group of the Galician army declares that the Galician army operates independently of Petliura's army under separate Galician command, without any political aims, and solely with the purpose of fighting the Bolsheviks"[13]. A day earlier, on August 30, at negotiations with the command of Denikin's 2nd brigade, its representatives demanded that the territory southeast and east of the Koziatyn - Fastiv - Kyiv railway be treated as "the operational zone of Denikin's troops", and that the strip between the line Fastiv - Volodarka - Saskiv - Buky and the line Bila Tserkva - Tarashcha - Lysivka - Zvenyhorodka be declared neutral; the diary records: "We did not agree to this demand"[13].

September 1994
C1193.T0008 →
C1193 First Russian-Chechen War (1994–1996)
G0011 Russian Federation
S0008 Government

In the autumn of 1994, Russia attacked the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria under the name of a puppet opposition, and on 11 December 1994 the Russian army openly invaded the republic. Russia did not recognize Ichkeria as an independent state, nor its elected president and government as a party to the war and to negotiations.
On 9 December 1994, two days before the open invasion, Chernomyrdin, chairman of the Russian government, by Resolution No. 1360 ordered the Ministry of Internal Affairs and the Ministry of Defense to disarm the "illegal armed formations" (as Russia called the Armed Forces of Ichkeria)[16]. On 17 December, Russian President Yeltsin, by Decree No. 2200, created a Russian administration for Ichkeria. On 20 December, the Russian government, by Resolution No. 1411, obliged all enterprises, organizations and institutions of the republic to carry out the decisions of this administration (see: "Installation of a New System of Governance")[17].
Throughout the war, the president, ministers and generals of Russia refused to negotiate with the leadership of Ichkeria or demanded that it first surrender its weapons and disband its army[17].
On 1 February 1995, Ilyushenko, acting Prosecutor General of Russia, charged Dzhokhar Dudayev, the elected president of Ichkeria, with high treason, calls for terrorism, violation of national and racial equality and violation of the law on referendums[18]. The Prosecutor General's Office of Russia issued a warrant for the arrest of the Chechen president and put him on the all-Russian wanted list[17].
On 30 July 1995, Russia signed an agreement with Ichkeria on the cessation of hostilities. The Russian delegation added to its first line that Ichkeria was not recognized by the legislation of Russia[19][20].
On 12 December 1994, the day after the open invasion, the Russian delegation at the negotiations in Vladikavkaz, the capital of North Ossetia, demanded that the representatives of Ichkeria recognize the republic as part of Russia[17].
On 17 December 1994, Dzhokhar Dudayev, the president of Ichkeria, informed Yeltsin by telegram that he had ordered a ceasefire against the Russian troops and proposed to begin negotiations without preconditions, which he would lead himself[21]. On 18 December, Yeltsin by telegram ordered the president of Ichkeria to appear that same day in Mozdok, a town in Russian-controlled North Ossetia, at the headquarters of the occupation troops and to sign a document on the surrender of weapons and a ceasefire[21][17]. The press service of the Russian president told the Russian news agency ITAR-TASS that further telegrams from the Chechen president would not be answered[22]. On 23 December, Dzhokhar Dudayev conveyed through Sergei Kovalyov, the Russian human rights commissioner, to Russian Foreign Minister Kozyrev that he was ready to cease fire immediately and to negotiate on everything, including the disarmament of the army of Ichkeria, but received no answer from Moscow[17]. On 27 December, Yeltsin, in a televised address to the citizens of Russia, declared: "the regime in Grozny [as the Russian president called the government of Ichkeria] is illegal", and of negotiations he said: "The subject of negotiations is a ceasefire and the surrender of weapons"[23][17]. On 31 December, the Russian army began the storming of Grozny, the capital of Ichkeria (see: "Military Intervention")[17].
On 18 January 1995, Russian President Yeltsin said on Russian television that he would not talk to Dzhokhar Dudayev[17]. From February to June 1995, Russian Defense Minister Grachev and Kulikov, the commander of the occupation troops, replied to the proposals of a ceasefire from Aslan Maskhadov, the chief of the Main Staff of the Armed Forces of Ichkeria, that they would talk only about the surrender of weapons and the disbanding of the army of Ichkeria[24][17][25]. On 19 May 1995, Yeltsin rejected the law by which, on 12 April, Russian deputies had instructed the Russian government to begin direct negotiations with the leadership of Ichkeria immediately and without preconditions[17][26]. On 5 July 1995, two weeks after negotiations with Ichkeria began in Grozny, Chernomyrdin, chairman of the Russian government, and the Russian negotiators declared that they would not discuss its status[17].
On 10 January 1996, Chernomyrdin, chairman of the Russian government, said: "Chechnya is Russia"[27]. On 11 January, the Russian government, in a letter to the UN Secretary-General, named the government of Ichkeria as the cause of the war, declaring it illegal and Ichkeria itself part of Russia[28]. On 7 April, Mikhailov, Russian minister for nationalities, said that the parties to the negotiations should be the commanders of the Armed Forces of Ichkeria. On 17 April, Russian Justice Minister Kovalyov declared that direct negotiations with Dzhokhar Dudayev, "a subject of criminal proceedings", were impossible, and invited him to come to a Russian court. On 21 April, the Russian military killed the president of Ichkeria (see: "Neutralization of the Opposition"). On 25 April, Justice Minister Kovalyov announced that negotiations with the commanders of the Armed Forces of Ichkeria would be conducted by the puppet government in Grozny[17].
On 27 May 1996, Russian President Yeltsin received in the Kremlin, for negotiations on a ceasefire, a delegation of Ichkeria headed by Zelimkhan Yandarbiyev, acting president of Ichkeria. The Russian organizers of the meeting assigned the representatives of Ichkeria seats at the side of the table, while Yeltsin sat at its head. Zelimkhan Yandarbiyev demanded to sit opposite the Russian president, as at negotiations between two states, and to Yeltsin's "Sit down" he replied across the negotiating table: "I will not sit down to that tone". The Russian president moved to the middle of the table[29]. The Russian delegation did not write the names of the parties into the ceasefire agreement, and Chernomyrdin, chairman of the Russian government, and Zelimkhan Yandarbiyev signed it with their surnames and no titles. Mikhailov, Russian minister for nationalities, explained this to journalists at a press conference at the Russian news agency Interfax: "If we had written 'Ichkeria', that would practically mean recognizing the existence of a republic that does not exist..."[29]. On 28 May, the Russian organizers of the meeting did not let the delegation of Ichkeria meet with Moscow journalists, and in the evening the delegation flew to Russian-controlled Ingushetia[17].
On 25 July 1996, General Pulikovsky, acting commander of the occupation troops, told journalists that with the commanders of the Armed Forces of Ichkeria he would discuss only the place and time of the surrender of weapons[30]. On 28 July, Pulikovsky replied on air on the Russian television channel RTR to Aslan Maskhadov's proposal to discuss ending the war that he did not know where to look for the leadership of Ichkeria[31][17].
Human rights defenders of Memorial write that the president and government of Russia called the war their internal affair[17].
On 31 August 1996, General Lebed, secretary of the Security Council of Russia, and Aslan Maskhadov, the chief of the Main Staff of the Armed Forces of Ichkeria, signed in Khasavyurt, a town in Russian-controlled Dagestan, the agreements on the withdrawal of the occupation troops (see: "Sham Treaties"). Russia and Ichkeria postponed the decision on the independence of Ichkeria for five years, until 31 December 2001[32][17]. The word "Ichkeria" does not appear in the agreements: the republic is called the Chechen Republic[32]. In September 1999, the Russian army invaded Ichkeria for the second time since 1994[17].
