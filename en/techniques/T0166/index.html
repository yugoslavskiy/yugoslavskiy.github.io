Manufacturing Criminals

Aggressors may manufacture "criminals" out of unwanted people: passing laws with offenses elastic enough to charge anyone ("counter-revolutionary activity", "extremism", "discreditation"), inventing conspiracies and underground organizations, forging "evidence", filling cases with people arrested by list, and keeping investigations and trials secret or turning them into public spectacle. Imprisonment becomes a matter of decision rather than fact, and every verdict retroactively "proves" the guilt of the colonized, justifying repression as a fight against "crime".

January 1927
C1178.T0166 →
C1178 Collectivization of Chechnya (1927-1936)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

The OGPU (the political police, a predecessor of today's FSB) fabricated the "Chechen nationalist center" - an underground organization that did not exist - and convicted thousands of Chechens in the case. Thus the empire "explained" the uprisings against the forced merging of households into kolkhozes ("collectivization"): it was not hunger and devastation that raised the people, but a secret conspiracy. The Chechen historian A. G. Avtorkhanov, who worked in Chechnya in those years and later wrote about it in emigration, described the mechanism of the fabrication: "The logic of the Chekists suggested that the culprits and organizers of all the anti-Soviet uprisings should be sought in some single 'nationalist center', into which, undoubtedly, by the same logic, representatives of the internal and foreign Chechen emigration must belong"[1]. The work, by his words, was taken up by the krai organ of the OGPU under Kursky and Fedotov and the Chechen oblast department of the OGPU under Kraft: "They created this 'counter-revolutionary nationalist center' so skillfully and plausibly that even many of the Chechens believed in its existence"[1]. The "evidence" was letters in the name of the Chechen émigré, oil industrialist Tapa Chermoev - with calls to prepare an uprising; they came to Chechen public figures in Grozny from Istanbul, Paris and London through intermediaries whom the OGPU had obliged to hand the letters over to it. Avtorkhanov concludes: "the version of Chermoev's letters was a lie, and the letters themselves - forgeries of the OGPU. But it was precisely these letters that served as 'material evidence' against the 'nationalist center'"[1]. In the autumn of 1932 the OGPU arrested the entire "group" of the addressees, followed by mass arrests across the Gudermes and Nozhay-Yurt districts - the very one that had revolted against the kolkhozes in March 1932; Avtorkhanov writes: "In all, up to 3 thousand people were arrested in the case of the 'Chechen nationalist center'. The arrested were charged with creating a 'counter-revolutionary nationalist center of Chechnya for the preparation and carrying out of an armed uprising'"[1]. There was no trial - Avtorkhanov writes: "Almost all the arrested were convicted through the collegium of the GPU. Of the members of the 'center', Abdulkadirov, Shamilev, Kurbanov, Motsa-Hadji Sotaev and Beymurzaev were shot. Others received 10 years each. How many of the rank and file were shot is impossible to establish"[1]. With the fabricated case the empire justified itself publicly as well: Yevdokimov, secretary of the krai committee of the party, at the krai party conference of 1934 cited "the letters of the millionaire Chermoev" as proof of the preparation of an uprising with the money and weapons of the Western powers[1]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov confirm the scale: repression "in connection with the case of the so-called 'Chechen nationalist center'" befell, "by some data, up to 3 thousand people"[2].

July 1937
C1179.T0166 →
C1179 The Great Terror in Chechen-Ingushetia (1937-1940)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

From the summer of 1937 to November 1938 the NKVD (the political police, a predecessor of today's FSB) arrested people in the republic by the categories and numbers assigned in operational order No. 00447 of Yezhov, the People's Commissar (minister) of Internal Affairs of the USSR, of 30 July 1937. The Chechen historian A. G. Avtorkhanov, who worked in Chechnya in those years and later wrote about it in emigration, described the beginning of the operation: "On the night of 31 July to 1 August 1937, by lists drawn up beforehand by the NKVD, the so-called 'general operation for the removal of anti-Soviet elements' was carried out across all auls and districts"; the Chekists hauled the arrested to Grozny - to the central garage of "Grozneft", to the Stalin club, to the pre-trial detention house of the republic's militia[1]. Avtorkhanov names "up to 14 thousand people" arrested in the first wave; the published operational summary on the course of the operation gives, for mid-August, about 2.4 thousand arrested across the whole republic, divided in it by the assigned categories: 888 "kulaks" (peasants whom the colonizer deemed "well-off"), 831 "criminals", 682 of the "counter-revolutionary element"[3]. Gridasov, head of the Gudermes district department of the NKVD, answered an officer who asked how to arrest a man when there was no material on him: "Material will always be found, so long as he wears a Caucasian hat!"[1]. The arrests went on until November 1938: in the autumn of 1937 Moscow raised the number assigned to the republic to 3,200 people, and on 1 March 1938 assigned a new one - 10,108 people, of whom the troika convicted 6,767[3]. On 8 September 1937 the newspaper "Pravda" printed an article by its special correspondent Kuzovkin under the headline: "The Bourgeois-Nationalist Tangle in Chechen-Ingushetia"[3]. Avtorkhanov relates its content: the author of the article "proved that in Chechen-Ingushetia 'bourgeois nationalists' sit in the party leadership and at the head of the government, leading the Chechen-Ingush people down an anti-Soviet path", and the newspaper and the author called on a judge not named by name: "to untangle the bourgeois-nationalist tangle to the end"[1]. Avtorkhanov writes of what the investigators manufactured out of the arrested: "Over the course of three years the Chekist investigators created the case of a 'bourgeois-nationalist, counter-revolutionary-insurgent, Bukharinite-Trotskyite, terrorist-espionage, anti-Soviet, wrecking organization' – literally so ran the many-storeyed formula of the charge for the top layer of the arrested"[1]. Into this "top layer", which the Chekists named the "bourgeois-nationalist center of Chechnya and Ingushetia", the investigation wrote 137 former senior officials of the republic. The investigation entered among these 137 also Abdurakhman Avtorkhanov, who was then studying in Moscow: it wrote people into the case regardless of where they were and what they did[2]. To each of them the investigation imputed Article 58 - treason against the Motherland, preparation of an armed uprising, wrecking, terrorist acts, diversion, anti-Soviet agitation, membership in an anti-Soviet organization, and sabotage. According to the charge, the "center" acted in alliance with identical "centers" of Dagestan, Ossetia, Kabardino-Balkaria, Karachay, Adygea and Circassia and was preparing, as Avtorkhanov writes, "the proclamation of a 'North Caucasus federative republic' under the protectorate of Turkey and England", with the "Moscow inter-national center" holding the liaison between them all[1].

September 1994
C1193.T0166 →
C1193 First Russian-Chechen War (1994–1996)
G0011 Russian Federation
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

In the autumn of 1994, Russia attacked the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria under the name of a puppet opposition, and on 11 December 1994 the Russian army openly invaded the republic. The occupiers detained Chechens as vagrants and forced them to sign confessions that they had fought in the Armed Forces of Ichkeria or helped them.

In 1995, in its orders for the detention of Chechens, the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs most often referred to decree No. 1815 of Russian president Yeltsin of 2 November 1993 "On Measures to Prevent Vagrancy and Begging"[4]. Most of the detained Chechens had their documents on them, and some of them the ministry took from their own homes[5]. The first thing the occupiers did was tear up their identity papers in front of their eyes[6].

Russian investigators demanded that the detainees confess to helping the army of Ichkeria and told them, blindfolded, to sign papers they could not see[6]. The occupiers extracted confessions by torture, including with electric current, in pits and prison vans at checkpoints and military units (see: "Torture and Abuse")[5].

The Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs released most of the detained Chechens without charges. According to a certificate by Zlydenko, deputy head of Russia's prison service, from 11 December 1994 to 22 July 1995, 1,325 people passed through the filtration camps for detainees in Mozdok, a town in Russian-controlled North Ossetia, and in Grozny. The ministry handed a hundred of them over to investigators (see: "Filtration Camp System")[5].

According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs, from 28 January 1995 to July 1996 the ministry charged 51 people of those who had passed through the camp in Grozny, released 1,024 and exchanged 109 for captured Russian servicemen[5].

On 1 February 1995, Ilyushenko, acting prosecutor general of Russia, charged Dzhokhar Dudayev, the elected president of Ichkeria, with high treason and incitement to terrorism on the basis of a telephone conversation which the prosecutors said they had intercepted[7]. The Prosecutor General's Office put the president of Ichkeria on a wanted list, and on 21 April 1996 the Russian military killed him near Gekhi-Chu, a village southwest of Grozny (see: "Neutralization of the Opposition")[5][8]. On 6 April 1995, the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs took 16 Chechens out of the filtration camps and forced each of them to sign a paper that called him a fighter of the army of Ichkeria, although, by their own account, none of them had fought. In Malye Atagi, a village south of Grozny, the ministry handed them over to the army of Ichkeria in exchange for a captured Russian major (see: "Hostage-Taking")[9].

On 15 March 1996, Russian soldiers at the checkpoint near Samashki, a village in the west of Ichkeria, detained 91 Chechens who were leaving the village before the assault. Russian investigators interrogated the detainees in the prisons of Pyatigorsk and Stavropol, cities in the south of Russia. One of them, Murat Ganiev, told Human Rights Watch on 14 October 1996: "I told them I wasn't a fighter. … They said that if I said I was a fighter they would release the other forty-six. The only thing they gave us to sign was our release papers"[10]. Five weeks after the detention, on 19 April 1996, the Russian Ministry of Internal Affairs released all the Chechens from Samashki without charges[5].

In a report of January 1997, Human Rights Watch wrote that the Russian government had not disclosed how many Chechens from the filtration camps it was holding in prison on charges of taking part in the fighting, or under which articles they had been tried[10].

By January 1997, when the last Russian troops had left Ichkeria, deputies of the Russian parliament had drafted an amnesty only for Russian servicemen. This draft did not allow the release of Chechens who were held in custody for taking part, or allegedly taking part, in the fighting[10]. On 12 March 1997, the deputies in Moscow, by resolution No. 1199-II, declared an amnesty for crimes connected with the war, but did not extend it to those charged and convicted under 17 articles of the Criminal Code, including for banditry and murder. The deputies allowed cases under these articles to be closed only against those whom Russia handed over in exchange for its own servicemen and citizens held in Ichkeria[11].
