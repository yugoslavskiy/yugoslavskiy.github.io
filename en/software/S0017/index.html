In January 1918, while the troops of Soviet Russia were advancing on Kyiv, the Bolsheviks raised an uprising inside the city itself. On Sunday 15 (28) January, workers and armed Bolshevik detachments seized the "Arsenal" plant in Pechersk (a district of Kyiv)[16]. On 19 January (1 February) Ordzhonikidze, the extraordinary commissar of Soviet Russia in Ukraine, telegraphed the government of Soviet Russia: "In Kyiv there is an uprising of workers and of soldiers of the Bohdan Khmelnytsky and Shevchenko regiments. The Arsenal is in the hands of the workers. Today Soviet troops will enter Kyiv"[18]. The uprising inside the city and the offensive from outside stand in one telegram and in one calculation.

T0022 Terror
January 1919
C0074 Soviet-Ukrainian War: Second Invasion and Resource Depletion (1919)
G0013 Soviet Russia (RSFSR)
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

In captured Kyiv the occupiers shot hundreds of people over 1919: they killed at night, printed the names of the dead daily in a newspaper, and in the last week of August proclaimed a "week of red terror." An eyewitness - Kateryna Koval-Medzvedska, a Kyivan who lived seven months in the city under Moscow's occupation, from February to August 1919 - recorded that on 24 August 1919 "a 'week of red terror' was officially proclaimed - blood flowed in rivers. Peters, Voroshilov and Latsis, all-powerful that day, took no account of government members Rakovsky, Manuilsky and others who signed orders for releases from the Cheka, but ordered those people shot 'in the order of red terror.'" The shooting was done at night: "From 2 to 4 o'clock the sinister night silence of the unhappy, tormented city was broken by single shots and the piercing sounds of a car siren. People living near one of the 17 chekas scattered across the city understood that our brothers were being shot, their cries and groans drowned out by the red executioners with the shrill piercing sounds of sirens"[19].

From 24 to 30 August inclusive the newspaper Izvestia - the organ of the Kyiv occupation authority - daily announced lists of dozens shot "in the order of red terror"; on 26 August "a list was announced of 167 persons shot 'in the order of red terror'" - among them people whose release order the eyewitness had read two days earlier in the chancellery of the central organ of the colonial administration (the VUTsIK). "And how many people perished without announcement, simply vanished without trace!"[19]. One of those newspaper lists survived in a reprint: Shumakher, acting head of the Whites' Information Section, reproduced in "Summary of Information on the Atrocities and Lawlessness of the Bolsheviks" No. 25 a list printed in the Kyiv Izvestia of people shot by the occupiers: a professor, a gymnasium teacher, merchants, house-owners, attorneys, former Kyiv governor Sukovkin, former vice-governor Neklyudov, 80-year-old Slynko[20]. The very form of the announcements came from Moscow: the RSFSR government's resolution "On Red Terror" of 5 September 1918, signed by justice minister Kursky and interior minister Petrovsky, required "publishing the names of all those shot, as well as the grounds for applying this measure to them" - the Kyiv lists with the formula "in the order of red terror" delivered names and grounds, exactly what the resolution demanded[21].

The head of the Soviet colonial administration of Ukraine, Rakovsky, wrote to Lenin on 9 August 1919 that the Kyiv secret police had been carried away with executions by hastily drawn-up lists and that more than 300 people had been shot in Kyiv in total[22], while Latsis, chairman of the Ukrainian secret police, gave his own statistics in his 1920 report book: "Shot by the Kyiv Extraordinary Commission jointly with the V.U.Ch.K. - 825" people in 1919 up to October[23].

T0162.003 Killing of Prisoners and Detainees
January 1919
C0074 Soviet-Ukrainian War: Second Invasion and Resource Depletion (1919)
G0013 Soviet Russia (RSFSR)
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

Retreating from Kharkiv in June 1919, the Bolsheviks' secret police killed its own prisoners and hostages - Ukrainians held in its jails without trial. By the count given in a summary of the Whites' Information Section, over the months of the Bolshevik occupation of Kharkiv more than 1,000 people were shot: 40-50 were killed daily, "and in the last days this figure rose sharply"; among the named dead were former governor Bantysh with his son, generals Nechaev and Kuskov, prince Putyatin[20]. Excavations after the Bolsheviks left yielded 239 bodies; forensic medical examination established facts of burial alive, of torment and of torture. In the concentration camp the secret police had set up on Chaikovska street, 33 bodies of executed hostages were dug up[20]. A witness from the camp hostile to the Whites - the left SR Karelin, held in the Kharkiv prison - confirmed both the routine and the last day: Saenko, commandant of the Kharkiv secret police, "usually shot all the condemned... with his own hand," and "on the last day of our stay in the Kharkiv prison... 120 people were shot in the back yard of our prison"[24]. There were no sentences: Kharkiv secret police investigators Portugeis and Feldman, by the documents studied by the historian Melgunov, "in 1919 shot people without any protocols at all" - by a pencilled resolution, "Baeva, as an incorrigible criminal, to be shot"[24]. Before leaving neighbouring Vovchansk the Bolsheviks shot 64 hostages, among them the headmistress of the girls' gymnasium[20]. In the settlement of Velykyi Burluk in the same Vovchansk district the Bolsheviks executed the entire family of prince Vadbolsky - nine people, among them two 80-year-old women and an English governess; the compilers of the summary recorded: "they were stripped naked, led behind the house church, candles were lit, they were ordered to kneel and pray, and then, to whistling and shouting, their ears were cut off, they were hacked with sabres and, as the victims lost consciousness, finished off with shots"[20].

T0162.004 Torture and Abuse
January 1919
C0074 Soviet-Ukrainian War: Second Invasion and Resource Depletion (1919)
G0013 Soviet Russia (RSFSR)
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

The Bolsheviks' secret police tortured detained Ukrainians in the captured cities: interrogations with beatings and torture regardless of sex or age, closet-cells and cellars flooded with water, staged executions, staff executioners paid per person shot. In Kyiv this was put on record by the Red Cross: the report of the Central Committee of the Russian Red Cross Society on the activity of the Kyiv secret police, compiled from the observations of nursing sisters over February - August 1919 and signed by the committee's acting chairman, doctor Lodyzhensky, describes the interrogations: "As soon as a person fell into the power of the ChK [the secret police], he lost all human rights... From the very first interrogation the screaming began... cruel beatings began, accompanied by abuse. Neither age nor sex protected from them" - "the fourteen-year-old daughter of the actress E. K. Chaleeva was cruelly beaten before her mother's eyes, to extract more candid testimony from both daughter and mother"; an investigator beat 60-year-old Vorovskaya in the presence of her arrested daughter[25]. The arrested Ukrainians were kept in a windowless cellar flooded with water and in a wall closet in which, according to the report, "one could only sit hunched." Interrogation was conducted by staged executions: Ustinsky "was led to the place of execution in the basement, undressed, laid on the floor... The shot did ring out, but calculated so that the bullet flew close but past. So close that... all the skin on Ustinsky's hands was scorched. Such shooting was repeated many times. In the end Ustinsky was shot dead"; Solntsev was forced to attend executions and locked in the basement as "the last living man among the still-warm corpses" - at a night interrogation he went mad, which, at the investigators' demand, was certified by the psychiatrist Kirichevsky, himself a prisoner. One of the sisters summed up seven months of observation thus: "there were no torn-out fingernails, no epaulettes nailed to shoulders, no flayed skin, no people scalded with boiling water. But their whole life was one continuous torture"[25].

In Odesa an eyewitness who had been held under arrest in the Odesa secret police and published his memoirs there in 1919 under the pseudonym K. Alinin recorded, from the words of the staff executioner Abash, how one of the chiefs of that secret police conducted interrogation: he "'broke a person up by parts.' He would usually sit down before his victim and begin questioning him. - An officer? - he would squint... and, taking aim with his revolver, shoot through the martyr's hand. - A colonel, perhaps? - And the bullet would shatter the elbow"; for each person shot, by the same Abash's words, the secret police paid the executioner[26].

In Kharkiv Saenko, commandant of the Kharkiv secret police, tortured the interrogated himself: his "favourite method," by testimony collected by the Whites' investigative commission, was this: "he would plunge a dagger a centimetre into the body of the person under interrogation and then turn it in the wound," and the torture went on "in the office of the investigator of the 'special department,' before the eyes of Yakimovich, his assistants and investigator Lyubarsky." The same was independently confirmed by the left SR Karelin, held in the Kharkiv prison: "Usually Saenko shot all the condemned with his own hand... In our presence in the Kharkiv prison he shot a sick man on a stretcher"[24].

T0099 Hostage-Taking
January 1919
C0074 Soviet-Ukrainian War: Second Invasion and Resource Depletion (1919)
G0013 Soviet Russia (RSFSR)
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

On 25 May 1919 Antonov-Ovseenko, commander of the RSFSR's Ukrainian Front, disarming the villages, ordered the armies: "beforehand, to take hostages from the kulak elements"[27]. On 29 June 1919 the central organ of the colonial administration of Ukraine (the VUTsIK) legalized "the taking of hostages" by its resolution on general mobilization[28], and on 11 December 1919 Trotsky, war minister of the RSFSR, included "the hostage system" in the list of means by which the armies "entering Ukraine" were obliged to disarm the population[29].

In Zhytomyr, during the nine days of Soviet rule in March 1919, hostages sat in the secret police "until payment of the contribution" - until the town paid the sum imposed on it; so recorded Livshits, commissioner of the Central Committee for Aid to Pogrom Victims, noting that a considerable part of those arrested "as hostages" were Jews[30]. So it was in Fastiv too: until the contribution imposed on the town was paid, Jews sat as hostages[31]. The occupation secret police in Kyiv held hostages as well: Rakovsky, head of the Soviet colonial administration of Ukraine, wrote to Lenin on 9 August 1919 that after the proclamation of "red terror" over the Hryhoriv uprising it had shot 30 hostages - former members of the monarchist organization "Union of the Russian People," which staged pogroms, and former tsarist officials; when it prepared to shoot 15 more and Rakovsky with Latsis, chairman of the Ukrainian secret police, checked their biographies, only two turned out to be dangerous enemies[22].

The removal of hostages from Kyiv during the evacuation at the end of August 1919 was described by the eyewitness Kateryna Koval-Medzvedska: "Doctors, engineers, topographers were put on record, and in the last days, under threat of execution, they were carried off by force to Moscow. Besides that, landowners and the bigger merchants were carried off by the hundreds as hostages"[19].

Everything else is open to question"[14]. In April 1942 the USSR cancelled the call-up of Chechens and Ingush altogether - Dementyev's certificate states: "In connection with this, in April 1942 there followed an order of the NKO of the USSR cancelling the call-up of Chechens and Ingush into the army"[49] (the NKO - the USSR ministry of defense). The Chechen historian A. G. Avtorkhanov, writing in emigration, dates the release from service to February 1942 and renders the order's motivation in the words "the Chechens and Ingush, by religious conviction, refuse to eat pork"[51]. The historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that it was the NKVD organs (a predecessor of today's FSB) that obtained the halt of the call-up in March 1942, and render the official motivation thus: "the halt of the call-up was motivated all but by care for the Chechens and Ingush, even by the impossibility of providing their nourishment with meat (the highlanders did not eat pork, and there was practically no mutton or beef laid in as canned food)"[14]. Two years later the USSR accused the people it had itself removed from the call-up of evading service and going over to the enemy; on this accusation the colonizer built the decree deporting the people from its own lands in 1944, known as Operation "Chechevitsa" ("Lentil") (campaign C1180) and later recognized as genocide of the Chechen people: by Russia in 1991[64], by the European Parliament in 2004[65] and by Ukraine in 2022[66].

T0121 Artificial Famine
February 1943
C1180 Operation "Chechevitsa": the Total Deportation of the Chechen People and the Special Settlement Regime (1943-1949)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

The USSR, deporting the Chechens and Ingush from their native land, made no provision for food at the places of exile - and from famine and disease in exile almost a third of the deported died within four and a half years. The Special Settlements Department of the Ministry of Internal Affairs tallied the toll for the Kazakh SSR: by 1 July 1949, of the Chechens, Ingush, Balkars and Karachays initially resettled there, 101,036 people had died - 23.3% of those brought in; "the highest mortality was among the deportees from the North Caucasus and the Kalmyks"[49]. The colonizer had taken the people's livestock and food back home: by the resolution of the State Defense Committee of the USSR of 31 January 1944 the people's commissariats (ministries) were obliged "to accept in the North Caucasus from the special settlers livestock and agricultural produce... issuing exchange receipts for what was accepted," while from the confiscated livestock 350 horses "fit for cavalry service" were handed over to the occupier's police cavalry units[69]. Pchelkin, People's Commissar (Minister) of Internal Affairs of the Kirghiz SSR, reported to People's Commissar (Minister) of Internal Affairs of the USSR Beria on 22 April 1944: the food the exiles had brought with them "is running out," "the established ration is critically insufficient," the collective farms are "in no position to help for lack of it, as a result of which in some districts there are instances... of the eating of roots and grasses"[49]. Historian P. M. Polian writes: for lack of food, shelter and fodder the exiles in Kirghizia "had to slaughter up to 90%" of the livestock issued "in compensation for that left behind in their former places of residence," while the funds allocated to them were handed out by local bosses to others - in the Kazalinsk district "one ton of bread was issued to the local party activists," and in the collective farms of the Bazar-Kurgan district "food was issued only to able-bodied special settlers, while those unfit for work were deprived of rations"[49]. In exile the Chechens and Ingush died by the tens of thousands: by the end of 1944 the number of exiles ("special settlers" in the colonizer's terms) had fallen by about 11.5 thousand in Kirghizia and by more than 46 thousand in Kazakhstan[14]. In 1945, among the North Caucasus exiles, 44,652 children died - 2,230 were born; no fewer than 80 thousand people were starving[14]; in the winter of 1948-1949 in the Kazakh SSR (today's Kazakhstan) up to 118 thousand were starving, and 418 people died of hunger[14]. By October 1948 about 146.9 thousand North Caucasus exiles had died in exile; historians put the direct losses of the Chechens and Ingush at 145.7 thousand people, and together with the unborn - at 300 thousand; the people recovered its pre-war numbers only by 1959[14].

T0142 Restriction of Settlement Geography
March 1949
C1181 "Exiled Forever": Life in Special Settlement and Return to an Occupied Homeland (1949-1965)
G0010 USSR
S0017 Secret Police and Security Services

The USSR forbade the exiled Chechens and Ingush to leave the district of settlement, which at times consisted of a few streets. A commission of the Central Committee of the party admitted this in 1953 in a memorandum to the head of government Malenkov: "the absence of a special settler without the appropriate permission beyond the limits of the district served by the special commandant's office... is regarded as escape and entails liability under criminal law"[76]. The exiled Ingush woman Aza Bazorkina, who at sixteen received a passport stamped "special settler," testifies how the commandant would not let her father's friend go to bury his dead wife: "The Muslim cemetery lay beyond the city limits, and he did not allow him to accompany his wife beyond that line"[67]. Only by its resolution of 5 July 1954 did the Council of Ministers of the USSR ease the regime: to those engaged in socially useful labor it granted "the right of residence within the given oblast, krai, republic"[77], but escape remained a crime: for leaving the settlement without permission people were tried under article 82 of the Criminal Code of the RSFSR (escape from a place of obligatory settlement)[76], and in 1956 "of 328 thousand Chechens, a little over 191.4 thousand were on the registers of the special commandant's offices"[14]. The ban on returning outlived even the decree of 9 January 1957: the minister of internal affairs of the USSR Dudorov asked the leadership of the Bolshevik party (the "Central Committee of the CPSU") to allow "persons of Chechen and Ingush nationality returning to their former place of residence without the permission of the Organizing Committee to be detained, removed from trains, steamers and other means of transport and returned to the places of their former settlement," and on 9 April 1957 reported: in three days "2,139 persons were identified and detained" on trains[49]. The Organizing Committee stretched the return out over four years and made it paid: the returnees complained to the formal head of state Voroshilov that the Organizing Committee's representative Nemukhin had in March 1957 "announced to us the procedure of our return over 1957-1960, with all expenses connected with the move charged to us, the movers ourselves"[49]; on 26 January 1957 the Organizing Committee and the bureau of the party's oblast committee (the "Grozny obkom of the CPSU") established that "arrival for permanent residence on the territory of the republic may take place only by permission of the Organizing Committee of the ChIASSR [the Chechen-Ingush republic]," and ordered the occupier's police to register newcomers only on those permits[49]; on 5 September 1960 the Council of Ministers of the RSFSR extended the deadline for return "until 1963"[49]. Mountain Chechnya remained closed: five mountain districts - Galanchozh, Cheberloy, Sharoy, Itum-Kale and Shatoy - the decree of 9 January 1957 did not restore[49]. Historians Ya. Z. Akhmadov and E. Kh. Khasmagomadov write that "the highlanders were forbidden to reside" there, while the repatriates "were settled in new state farms, workers' settlements, farmsteads... especially in the Cossack districts beyond the Terek, and in arid, unhealthy places remote from district centers and towns"[14]. The same historians write that in the stanitsas, along the way, "conditions were created for their rapid cultural and linguistic assimilation": "In the stanitsas teaching was conducted only in Russian; the heads of stanitsa soviets, districts and farms remained exclusively Russians"[14]. The pogrom of August 1958 in Grozny, with the demand from the pogromists' resolution "to allow the Chechen-Ingush population to reside in Grozny oblast at no more than 10% of the total population"[49], the USSR turned against the Chechens themselves. The historians write: "In November 1958 a special passport regime was established in Grozny and in the Grozny (rural) district, under which serious restrictions on residence registration were introduced here... the true purpose of this action was to create administrative obstacles in the way of the migration of the Chechen population into Grozny," and "The initiators of the introduction of the ban, under the pretext of tightening the fight against a rise in offenses, were the party and soviet organs of Chechen-Ingushetia"[14].
